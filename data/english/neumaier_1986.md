# Neumaier \[1986\]

## **Die Herren von Rosenberg**

Bemerkungen zur frühen Geschichte einer fränkischen Niederadelsfamilie  
von HELMUT NEUMAIER

Often and extensively, our investigations had to cross today's borders to search for material from Baden and Bavaria; here and there, we also hoped to have contributed something useful for our neighbors. Usually, this happened by chance, but today we intentionally set out to give due honor to the first name of our association (Note: 'Historical Association for Franconian Wirtemberg and its Borders'), and we seek a topic that is of equal interest to our Württemberg Franconia and its two neighboring lands; a topic that is capable of promoting provincial or local history on both sides of the borders. In this search, the wealthy family of the Lords of Rosenberg soon presents itself to us, who owned a series of castles and manors in present-day Baden and Bavaria, as well as in our Württemberg, and who play a not insignificant role in provincial history.

The citation of Hermann Bauer¹ is intended as an appreciation of a historian whose thematic concern was the research of the lower or knightly nobility, and who methodologically did not let himself be deterred by state borders—for a long time, there was still a Baden, Bavarian, etc., nationality\! It is in the nature of things that the research questions have changed since his 1872 study on the Rosenbergs appeared. The genealogical and prosopographical approach has given way to the clarification of fundamental questions of social structure and the effects of social change². In many respects, however, we are still dealing with the same problems that H. Bauer already raised. Genealogy is not the least among them. Every investigation into the early history of a lower noble family must be accompanied by the awareness that its formation took place in a period of limited literacy³, within which the high or free nobles⁴ adopted written form for legal transactions relatively late, and the ministerial nobility even later. The transition from a single name to a family name⁵ occurred with a similar time lag, albeit with considerable variations within the same social stratum. An early example for the ministerials are the Boxberg servants Berthold and Hartmann von Schweigern before 1120 in the Komburg book of traditions⁶. This phenomenon is the second prerequisite for both noble strata to become tangible at all. Only from the second half of the 13th century do lower nobles show clear contours as a family. One must confine oneself to comparatively modest questions: When and in what ruling context can the Rosenbergs first be identified? What did their property base look like? In what way did their expansion beyond their ancestral seat take place? Behind these, it is not difficult to identify problems of a more significant nature: What prerequisites enabled them to rise to the top group⁸ of the lower nobility? Why did they succeed and not other families within what was, based on connubium, a sociologically homogeneous stratum? What opportunities did the political structures of Franconia offer for such an ascent, or were they able to hinder it? To what extent do personal abilities play a role? The thematic limitation to a single noble house should not lead one to expect (or more precisely: to force) answers. The question of origin thus seemed to be resolved. The Rosenbergs take their name from the village located a few kilometers east of Osterburken, and therefore their ancestral seat must have been located there. It is now known that in most noble families, the family name remains fixed, but there are certainly deviations from this name constancy. Walther Möller10 objected to Bauer, arguing that the Uissigheimers did not descend from the Rosenberg line, but rather the opposite was true. Based on somewhat broader source material than was available to both researchers, Möller's thesis is confirmed.  10 Walther Möller: Stamm-Tafeln westdeutscher Adels-Geschlechter im Mittelalter, Vol. 2, 1933, p. 188\.

An indication of their connection may also be seen in their burial place. Both the Uissigheimers and the Rosenbergs, the latter until the first half of the 14th century, found their final resting place in Bronnbach Abbey13, although the much closer Schöntal Abbey, near Rosenberg castle and village, would have been a more likely choice. These are admittedly no more than clues, but three other facts must be taken particularly seriously: their shared property in and around Uissigheim—the Rosenbergs still had possessions there in 1381 and held the parish patronage until their line died out in 163214—, the frequency of their joint appearances in documents, and especially the combination of names such as de Rosinberg dictus de Ussinkeim (of Rosenberg called of Uissigheim). The awareness of a common descent remained alive for a long time. To give an example: Knight Arnold the Younger of Uissigheim was exiled from the territory for ten years by Count Rudolf IV of Wertheim. The status designations liber and vir nobilis can only refer to free or high nobles, never to ministerials. The Uissigheimers who witness the 1260 document undoubtedly belong to the lower nobility. This raises the occasionally invoked problem of "Entfreiung" (loss of free status). This term describes the reduction in status resulting from the marriage of a free noblewoman to a member of the lower nobility20. In the case of the Uissigheimers, this cannot be ruled out from the outset, as there is a striking continuity, especially in the first names21.   20 On this, see R. Consequently, one must assume that a distinction is to be made between a free noble family and a lower noble family, both named von Uissigheim. After 1247, all trace of the nobiles is lost, so one may assume their extinction. Although there is no evidence of a connection with the Counts of Wertheim, a kinship link to this comital house might have existed. Indeed, Wertheim property is found in a conspicuous concentration in the immediate vicinity of Uissigheim.

More difficult is the question of when and why the lower nobles split into two main branches. The Uissigheim line died out in 1546 with Martin, the Rosenberg line in 1632 with Albrecht Christoph. We met Eberhard and Arnold von Uissigheim in 1260, and in 1285 Eberhard Rufus (the Red) called himself von Rosenberg. The separation of the two family branches must have begun during this period.

First, let us present the source findings. On January 14, 1285, Count Rudolf II of Wertheim sold four farms in Kirschfurt to Bronnbach Abbey24. The legal act was witnessed by Eberhardus Rufus de Ussenkeim and his brother Conradus iuvenis (the Younger), then Eberhardus vicedominus de Ussenkeim and his brother Fridericus Stahel. In that same year, the count sold a mill located in Reicholzheim to the abbey²⁵. This document names Eberhardus Rufus de Rosenberg and Conradus iuvenis, as well as the brothers Eberhardus quondam vicedominus (Eberhard formerly vicedominus) and Fridericus Stahel, the latter two as milites de Ussinkeim (knights of Ussinkeim). Thus, Eberhard Rufus, who is named once after Rosenberg and once after Uissigheim, is the same person. Furthermore, it is obvious that by 1285 the property in the Bauland had already been acquired. Three years later, Abbot Wintherus of Bronnbach settled a dispute over meadows in Hemsbach between Seligental Abbey and Eberhardus miles de Rosinberg dictus de Ussinkeim (Eberhard, knight of Rosenberg called of Ussinkeim)26.   24 StA Wertheim Lit. Thus, while the two main lines of the Rosenbergs can be traced with sufficient certainty after the turn of the 13th/14th century, the genealogical connection with the Uissigheim line remains highly uncertain. Although some individuals are known, they are difficult to place. The uncertainty also lies in the unresolved generational alignment, as shifts could well have occurred due to different lifespans. The following can therefore only be regarded as an attempt.

The starting point is the fratres (brothers) Eberhard and Arnold von Uissigheim mentioned in the Rieneck sales document of 1260\. Let us assume that Eberhard is identical with the vicedominus. The document was witnessed by representatives of both legal parties. For this, Mainz probably drew on individuals who held a function in the Archbishopric. That this was particularly true for its representative in Aschaffenburg, adjacent to the Spessart, is self-evident. With Fridericus Stahel, one would then have a third brother.

In summary, the connection could have been that around 1260, four or five family branches existed. Two of them were the starting points for those who, in some way, acquired Rosenberg.

We now return to these Uissigheim-Rosenbergs. The center of their domain was the castle and village, called a town in 137845. To the west and north of it were grouped the fiefs of the Prince-Bishopric of Würzburg in Götzingen, Bofsheim, Sindolsheim, and Osterburken. This also included the church patronages (with the exception of those in Sindolsheim and Götzingen). When Bishop Gottfried III (1314-1322) enfeoffed Arnold II with Hirschlanden46, an expansion of possessions to the east began. Eberhard reached the peak of his influence under Archbishop Heinrich von Virneburg (1328-1346). In 1346, the latter appointed guardians to govern the archbishopric. Among the lay members was the Vogt of Walldürn. The remuneration of 100 pounds of Heller⁵² was an income far exceeding the amounts a lower noble family could expect. In general, service to Mainz meant income in cash. Thus, in 1350, it turned out that the archbishop had been unable to pay the Rosenberg for providing eight armed men. In return, the income from Mudau in the Odenwald was assigned to him⁵³. The appointment as Vogt of Wildenberg marks the pinnacle of his influence. On November 7, 1354, Archbishop Gerlach of Nassau appointed Eberhard Rüdt as Vogt of Wildenberg⁵⁴. The Rosenberg must have died in that year. The money that the archbishopric owed him was advanced by Rüdt. It is known that there were stratifications within the lower nobility⁵⁹. Without a doubt, the Rosenbergs belonged to the top group. This fact, however, does not explain the prerequisites that enabled them to reach this position. One must be limited to citing one or another possible explanation. The rise to supra-regional importance began with Eberhard III (II) in the service of the Archbishopric of Mainz. His field of activity has been described in some detail because it exemplifies the opportunities available to a member of the lower nobility. If one starts from the well-known principle that proximity to the king brings power, this can certainly be transferred to a lower level. Proximity to the princely court was suited to gaining prestige and influence. Assignments of revenue and cash income gave the Vogt a financial leeway that cannot be assumed for all his peers by any means. Personal qualities were indispensable. Above all, Eberhard must be credited with a considerable degree of political astuteness. Anyone who played such an important role at the Archbishopric of Mainz, was able to gain a foothold with the Palatinate rival at the same time, and also knew how to assert himself in the field of tension between Mainz and Würzburg—Eberhard held fiefs from the prince-bishopric in Schweinberg⁶⁰—can be certified as having a fine sense for power relations.  59 R. One would very much like to know whether the Uissigheim-Rosenbergs had already produced personalities of such stature in the preceding generations. Here, one should recall the vicedominus of the Archbishopric in Aschaffenburg, Eberhard von Uissigheim. One may assume that seeking proximity to princes was already a tradition in the noble house before it split. Perhaps his biography finds parallels in that of the Vogt of Walldürn, and it cannot be ruled out that the financial foundation was laid here that enabled the descendants to purchase Rosenberg.

The fief books of the Prince-Bishopric of Würzburg list possessions that form a ring around Rosenberg castle and village. Conspicuously, the castle and village themselves are missing. The very first entry stating that the castle and village were given as a fief is found under the date October 18, 1380, in the fief book of Bishop Gerhard von Schwarzburg61. This date and another are so close together that coincidence can be ruled out. Namely, on May 25, 1381, Konrad VIII (VI) and Konrad VII (V), as well as the brothers Eberhard IX (VI) and Arnold II (II) von Rosenberg, purchased the commandery of Boxberg from the completely indebted Bailiwick of Franconia of the Knights of St. John62. On that very day, the Electors Palatine acknowledged that they had given and paid a sum of money to one of the Rosenbergs for this purpose63. The consideration consisted of making a quarter of Rosenberg and the entire new acquisition of Boxberg a fief⁶⁴. The coincidence of events suggests that the entrusting of the other three quarters of Rosenberg is to be seen against the same background. It must be admitted that some of these considerations are somewhat uncertain. However, they offer a plausible explanation for movements of property and lordship that have hitherto received little attention.

An attempt was made to expand or at least to specify some details of the state of knowledge on the early history of the Rosenbergs since H. Bauer.  1\. The thesis, assumed by H. Bauer and substantiated by W. Möller, of an identity between the Uissigheimers and the Rosenbergs is confirmed.  2\. A distinction must be made between the high noble family of Uissigheim, which died out in the mid-13th century, and the lower nobles of the same name. The latter become tangible in 1260 and probably originally belonged to the ministerialage of the free or high nobles.  3\. Who the descendants of the brothers Eberhard and Arnold were remains hypothetical, as does the progeny of some other Uissigheimers belonging to this generation.  4\. In the second half of the 13th century, four to five family branches can be expected, whose genealogical connection is partly unclear.  5\. With Eberhard Rufus and the children of Arnold von Uissigheim, it was possible to extend the Rosenberg family tree backwards by one generation.  6\. With them begins the division of the Uissigheimers into two main branches, one of which continued the old name, while the other took up that of Rosenberg Castle.  7\. The background of this acquisition is the decline of the free nobles of Dürn.  8\. The family tree created by W. Möller, which was based on the distinction between an "older (Eberhardian)" main line and an "Arnoldian" one, can possibly be corrected to the effect that there were two main lines \- an older one descending from Eberhard Rufus, and a younger one that split with Eberhard III (II) and Arnold I, but inherited from the former. All subsequent Lords of Rosenberg descend from it.  9\. A prerequisite for belonging to the top group of the Franconian lower nobility, and thus also for having extraordinary financial means, was service to the powerful. The biography of Eberhard III (II) serves as an illustration of this.  10\. Based on the name and the property situation, the Mönch family, who were not a side branch of the Uissigheim-Rosenbergs, are to be considered the former owners of the castle and village. The change of ownership is to be dated to the years before 1285\.  11\. The castle and village were acquired as allodial property. Their conversion into a fief is connected with the purchase of the Boxberg commandery in 1381\.

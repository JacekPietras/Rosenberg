# Bauer [1872]

## **Württembergisch Franken. [[source]](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_TJUEAAAAQAAJ/page/186/mode/2up)**

Journal of the Historical Society for Württemberg Franconia. Ninth Volume, Second Issue. Year 1872.

**I. Treatises and Miscellanea. 1. The Lords of Rosenberg.** *Von H. Bauer.*

Our memorandum to celebrate the 25th anniversary of the Historical Society for Württemberg Franconia has recalled that the society originally adopted the name: "Historical Society for Franconian Württemberg and its Borders."

Often and much, our investigations have had to cross today's borders and seek material from Baden and Bavaria; here and there, we hope to have contributed something useful for our neighbors as well. Usually, this has happened quite by chance, but today we are deliberately aiming to do due honor to that first name of our society, and we seek a subject that holds equal interest for our Württemberg Franconia and its two neighboring countries; one that is capable of promoting provincial or local history on both sides of the borders. In this search, the richly endowed family of the Lords of Rosenberg soon presents itself to us, who, in today's Baden and Bavaria, as well as in our Württemberg, possessed a series of castles and possessed knightly estates and played a not insignificant role in provincial history. I will briefly recall the knightly estates of Rosenberg, Boxberg, and Schüpf in Baden, the lordship of Niedernstetten, and the knightly estate of Waldmannshofen; the co-ownership of Jagstberg, Bartenstein, Neudeck, Urhausen, Maienfels, etc., in Württemberg; and the ownership of Röttingen, Aub, Gnötzheim in Bavaria.

Biedermann, in his Ritteranton Ottenwald, provides the genealogy of the already extinct Lords of Rosenberg of his time, and he had good materials at hand. Unfortunately, however, the use of these materials is often incorrect, making everything uncertain, and his documentary reports often lose their usefulness because he used them too hastily and distorted them with his own additions.

I am not aware of more recent works. In Mone's Zeitschrift des Oberrheins IX, 1, two different families of Rosenberg—the monks of Rosenberg and the simple Lords of Rosenberg (their genealogy is as different as their coat of arms)—were erroneously confused with each other. Although this error was corrected in volume X, 1, pp. 120 ff., a new mistake was made by declaring the Lords of Rosenberg to be dynasts, which they never were. They were knightly lords, ministerials, and vassals of the dynasts and bishops in the surrounding area [see Wirtemb. Franken 1859, p. 73 ff.]

The Lords of Rosenberg, whom Biedermann claims to know since the 10th century, are merely empty names from the tournament books; we do not bother to list them here. Adelheid von Neydeck, the wife of Conrad von Rosenberg, who is recorded in the Brombacher Necrologium (in the Unterfränkisches Archiv, Volume XXI) under July 22 with the year 1227, certainly belongs to another century. So, we are left with only a Hans von Rosenberg, who — if one trusts Biedermann — is mentioned in documents from the years 1271, 1286, and 1290. However, we have not encountered him anywhere.

On the other hand, the cited Brombacher Necrologium (see also Unterfränkisches Archiv XX, 3, p. 161) lists an Aplo von Rosenberg, who died on September 11, 1278, and ~~according to the Regestis boicis IV, 15 f., Ekehardus dictus Rosenberg in 1276 transferred fiefs to the Bishopric of Würzburg in Durreback, Bagenleiten, Schatenowe. This might well be—in the corrupted form of the name—the Eckelinus de Rosenberg who is mentioned in the document of 1287 Rübel III, 74 among the Würzburg vassals.~~ [Confused with Eckard von Rossy]. According to Biedermann's specific and therefore likely document-based information, Table 402, Eckhard von Rosenberg and his brother Eberhard made an agreement with each other in 1312.

This Eberhardus de Rosenberg is also mentioned in the already cited Brombacher Necrologium as having died on October 12, 1314; see Unterfränkisches Archiv, XXI. However, this Eberhard bore a special surname according to the following document from 1288: Conradus—Abbot in Amerbach—and Eberhardus, knight of Rosenberg, called "de Ussinkheim," acknowledge that their dispute with the monastery of Seligenthal over certain meadows in Heimesbach has been settled under the mediation of Abbot Wintherus de Brunnebach and Egeno, priest in Dürne. The nuns ceded to Eberhard von Rosenberg a meadow that they had jointly possessed with him in Sindelsheim. Gudeni C. dipl. III, 715 f. Reg. b. IV, 393.

Considering that the Lords of Ussigheim bore the same coat of arms as the Lords of Rosenberg, there can be little doubt: this Eberhard founded a distinct lineage at the castle in Ussigheim, and the subsequent Lords of Ussigheim also belong to the Rosenberg family tree in a broader sense.

Of course, Biedermann's information in Canton Rhön und Werra, Tables 433 and 434, does not align with this, according to which an Eberhard von Ussigheim, along with a brother named Arnold, appeared in a document as early as 1247; Arnold von Ussigheim again in 1276 as ministerialis herbipolensis and a Conrad von Ussigheim in 1280 and 1303, likewise in 1299 Conrad Lesch, so called in Ussigheim, and in 1299 and 1301, C. Lesch dictus de Ussenkeim miles [Württemberg Franconia 1848, Appendix, pp. 10-11]. We want to believe that specific information about a document from 1247, but it can be easily reconciled with our above interpretation if we assume that the inheritance of those brothers from Ussigheim passed through an heiress, along with their family's favorite name, to the Lords of Rosenberg, from whom a branch soon took residence in Ussigheim.

Eberhard von Rosenberg at Ussigheim could likely have had two sons, according to the cited Brombacher Necrologium: Conradus de Ussigheim, whose wife Gutta died on March 1, 1311, and Frizo de Ussigheim, noble, who died on October 5, 1324, with his wife Petrissa, who died on September 13, 1325. (The distribution in Biedermann's family tree is, of course, also his own interpretation. However, since all the later Lords of Ussigheim bear the Rosenberg coat of arms, they naturally all descend from the Lord of Rosenberg who relocated to Ussigheim. The conclusion of Biedermann's family tree can be significantly improved by our annual publication from 1857, p. 224).

Returning to the Lords of Rosenberg, we find in the next generation, without a doubt, a son of Eckard, a Conrad von Rosenberg, who in 1315 witnessed a donation by Count Rudolf von Wertheim in Nuremberg [Reg. b. V, 323, and in the same year, l. c. V, 308.]

In 1316, he was granted permission to redeem some Dürnesche fiefs in Benzenweiler, Merchingen, and in the town of Selhech (?) that had already been pledged to the Schönthal Monastery in 1313. [see Jahrbesh. 1847.]

Conrad seems to have died early, but he left behind sons. In 1321, an Arnoldus de Rosenberg, knight, along with Eberhardus and Wipertus, his fraternal brothers, exchanged a wine tithe with the monastery of Seligenthal [see Gud. C. D. III, 738 f.] These are certainly a brother of Conrad and the children of his brother. With them, the Rosenberg family line now diverges into two main branches. We will first attempt to identify the main figures of these two family branches or genealogical lines, as far as possible; various individual notes can then be supplemented. However, even establishing the family tree presents significant difficulties, especially because the records we have gradually collected do not contain sufficient genealogical information, while the constant recurrence of the same first names makes the correct interpretation and classification of many notes extraordinarily difficult. We have made and repeatedly revised 10–12 drafts of a family tree, as shortcomings have continually come to light. Our current family tree is certainly the most satisfactory relative to the others, but we promise in advance our heartfelt thanks for any further genealogical material*) that will enable us to further complete the family tree.

*) It will be welcome for the annual publication. Hartmann.

We begin with the previously mentioned Conrad von Rosenberg, 1315 and following, who died in 1321, and his sons Eberhard and Wipert. We have not encountered Wipert again, and it remains a very uncertain possibility that we tentatively place a Diether von Rosenberg, knight, who is mentioned in a solitary (Amorbacher document) in 1343, in the family tree as perhaps his son or, preferably, as another brother. His descendant could be the Eberhard von Rosenberg, Commander of the Order of St. John at Aichen in 1368, whom Biedermann lists in Table 402. Eberhard I von Rosenberg appears much more frequently (later followed by Arnold's son, Eberhard II); and then, in turn, each has a son bearing the father's name, Eberhard III and IV. Eberhard I is mentioned as a witness in a document from 1325 in [Wentz's Hessische Geschichte, II, 303]. In 1327, he sold two estates in Sindolsheim to the Brombach Monastery for 70 Hellers, [Reg. b. VI, 231] and to the Schönthal Monastery two mills, one near Rosenberg and the other between Sindolsheim and Rosenberg. His wife, Lady Irmentrurt, is also mentioned in this context.

In 1329, Lord Kraft von Hohenlohe reconciled with Eberhard von Rosenberg regarding a castle peace at Nagelsberg, where Eberhard had somehow acquired the castle. A bit later, for example, in 1334, Eberhard von Rosenberg appears as the Mainz bailiff at Dürne (Walldürn) (Gud. C. D. V, 352), and these two characteristics provide us with a guide to distinguish this Eberhard I from his contemporaries of the same name. We summarize here:

First, a note from an episcopal Würzburg fief book. Eberhardus de Rosenberg, knight, receives ex resignatione Dietheri Ruede two parts of the major tithe of the village of Boffsheim purchased from the same; likewise, the patronage rights of the churches of Rosenberg, Burkheim, Boffsheim, and ¼ of the village of Boffsheim with its appurtenances; likewise, the tithe in Sindolsheim, likewise there ¼ of the tithe called "das Metelsheim";*) likewise, the village of Bremen with advocacy and its appurtenances; and judgment there; likewise, ¼ of the village of Buch with advocacy and judgment and other appurtenances; likewise, in the village of Schweinberg two parts of the tithe. Year 1333. *) In 1353, the "Mettelzehntlein," where Eberhardus de R., knight, was newly enfeoffed with the same properties.

In 1334, Knight Eberhard von Rosenberg conducted business for Archbishop Baldwin of Mainz in Amöneberg, Geismar, and Fritzlar; Reg. b. VII, 84.

In 1335, he is occasionally referred to as a fidelis, a vassal of the Bishop of Würzburg, and this bishop wrote to Knight Eberhard, instructing him to protect the Romburg Monastery in his fiefdom over the castrum Nagelsberg, from which the same Lord Kraft von Hohenlohe had expelled the monastery; Wibel IV, 43. Among the guarantors in the sale of the lordship of Scheuerberg was also Lord E. von R. in 1335. In 1339, Lord E., the Archbishop's bailiff at Düren, acted as a witness for Mainz; Reg. b. VII, 260, and was seated at a Mainz fief court, Gud. C. D. I, 954. In 1342, E. de R. is mentioned as a knight and advocate in Durne, Reg. b. VII, 343, and in a 1343 document from Amorbach, E. von R. is referred to as the Bauth (bailiff) of Durn. In 1345, Biedermann reports in Table 402 the fiefs he received: two parts of the tithe in Boffsheim, the patronage rights in Burgheim, Rosenberg, and Boffsheim, the great and small tithes in Sindolsheim and ¼ Mettelscheinlein, the village of Bramen with advocacy and all jurisdiction, ¼ of the village of Buch likewise, and two parts of the tithe in Schweinberg.

1347: E. von R., the bailiff at Dürne, has under his authority the serfs of the empire in the Odenwald and on the Kocher; D.A. \= Description of Dehringen, pp. 158 and 142; he is referred to as the guardian of the Mainz chapter, Reg. b. VIII, 104; in 1349, he permitted the Archbishopric of Mainz to redeem the Nagelsberg Castle for 400 ß Heller; Reg. b. VIII, 172. At this time, Eberhard was a supporter of the antipope Bishop Heinrich von Birnenburg, Gud. C. D. III, 344; in 1354, Knight E. had a negotiation with the chapter in Mainz regarding debt documents; Reg. b. VIII, 289.

This concludes our records, but fortunately, a document provides information about his descendants. In 1341, Eberhard and Konrad, brothers of Rosenberg, sons of Lord Eberhard von Rosenberg of Durn—promise not to overburden the King's people, whom their father handed over to them and their brothers Engelhard and Hermann, with unjust levies and the like. In the event of a complaint, their mutual friends Eberhard, Konrad, and Hans (incorrectly printed as Heinz; they are the sons of Arnold von Rosenberg), all three named von Rosenberg, shall pass judgment. Reg. boic. VII, 298.

Eberhard, the son of Lord Eberhard, could be the E. von R. referred to as "von Zimmern" (likely Zimmern near Grünsfeld), who in 1340 ceded his tithe in Wülfingsloch to the Brombach Monastery with the consent of the Count of Wertheim; see our annual publications from 1857, p. 222, and 1869, p. 400. This Eberhard could also be the "Eb. de R. armiger junior" with his wife Wyclo, who sold property in Kirchheim (near Zimmern) to the Himmelspforten Monastery for 105 ß Heller. Among others, Joannes de Rosenberg sealed the transaction; Reg. b. VII, 306 f.

All things considered, however, we find it more likely that E. von R. with his wife Wyclo, who resided in Zimmern, is the son of Arnold and the father of Eberhard IV, the knight and bailiff at Lauda, who died in 1388; see below. In contrast to this Eberhard IV, Eberhard III, the nobleman, who, for example, guaranteed a bond for Mainz in 1363, Reg. b. IX, 81, is certainly the elder, and thus he is undoubtedly the "Junker" E. von R., "the elder," who, for example, witnessed documents of the Teutonic Order in 1380 and 1385. In 1383, Eberhard von Rosenberg the elder relinquished to Anna Landschabin, his lawful wife, all his possessions in Breigzheim near Hardheim, including court rights and other properties; likewise, all his properties in Sindolsheim in the Zorn, for 1200 florins, which she had proven to him. The bearer of this was Guntz Münch, bailiff at Steinsberg. He reappears in 1407, on the Tuesday after Palm Sunday. Bishop Johann of Würzburg enfeoffed, at the request of Eberhard von Rosenberg the elder, his son Ulrich with the following fiefs, which had been called back: Eberhard von Rosenberg's share in Boffsheim, a portion of the tithe in Meckmühl, ½ of the tithe in Brunacker, his share and holdings in Breigzheim, his share in the small tithe in Rosenberg, and the small tithe and his holdings in Burtheim With that, we lose his trail. The son Ulrich received the aforementioned fiefs in 1407, 1412, and again in 1418 (in 1412 also a share of the tithe in Zimmern and his share in Higelsdorf?). We also find this Ulrich von Rosenberg in 1408 and 1411 in the Reg. b. XII, 17 and 89, as a seal bearer in 1411 and as an assistant to the burgraves against Rothenburg before 1408. In 1421 and 1423, Anselm von Rosenberg—Ulrich's son, who probably also had an (older) brother named Michel—received the fiefs. Michel, Ulrich's son, also once received his share in Boxberg from the Palatinate; unfortunately, we do not know the exact year.

This Anselm provided surety for 500 florins in 1439; see Hessisches Archiv XI, 104, and sold a part of the castle at Schüpft to Eberhard, Götz, and Wilhelm von Dottenheim, which was confirmed by the regional court. In 1448, Anselm received his share of the villages of Boffsheim and Breitgheim, the tithe at Brunacker, Zimmern, and Higelsdorf. To free the tithe at Meckmühl, he enfeoffed his share of the two hamlets of Ersalt. From 1443 to 1454, he held the castle, town, and office of Röttingen in pawn, from where, in 1454, he launched an attack on merchants from Nördlingen traveling to the Frankfurt Fair. That same year, he took his own life by hanging himself (Wibel III, 109.*), and the bishop then seized his estate as forfeited, even though Anselm left behind a daughter, Kunigunde, who was later married to a Vesseer. Biedermann names him Hans Vesseer of Rothenburg; Table 405. The brothers Georg, Arnold, and Friedrich von Rosenberg took care of their cousin, demanded compensation, and lodged a complaint with the Diocese of Würzburg until the bishop agreed to a settlement in 1487 and paid 7500 florins; see Chmel, Kaiser Friedrich III., no. 8029. However, it seems that the dispute was not yet fully resolved, as the Unterfränkisches Archiv XIV, 2, p. 160 mentions a 1496 printed deduction by Bishop Rudolf of Würzburg against the Lords of Rosenberg in this matter.

Let us return to Eberhard (the elder) and his brothers, of whom Hermann, according to Biedermann's Table 402, became a canon at Würzburg.

The following records concern these brothers:

In 1353, the Lord Bishop of Würzburg acknowledged to Lady Guta, wife of Engelhard von Rosenberg, knight, a claim over ½ of the tithe in Boffsheim for her dowry of 200 florins, to be held until redeemed by the said Engelhard for the same amount of florins, and her brother C. von Bickenbach should bring it to her. Likewise, the Lord Bishop acknowledged to Elisabeth, wife of Conrad von Rosenberg, knight, a claim over the same tithe also for her dowry of 200 florins... and Johann von Seckendorf, brother of Elisabeth, should bring it to her in the meantime. (Würzburg Fief Book).

Engelhard and Conrad von Rosenberg, knights, brothers, sold all their own people in Mergentheim to the said lords and renounced all rights to them in 1356.

In 1359, Engelhard von Rosenberg, armiger, received ½ of the tithe in Rugheim from the resignation of Aplonis Fuchs and Ditricus, knights, who had held it faithfully for the mother of Otto Fuchs, and Otto himself resigned his portion as it pertained to him. (Würzburg Fief Book).

A son of Engelhard is mentioned again in a Würzburg fief note of uncertain date, as 1353 in our transcript seems too early for a married son.

13 . . ? Engelhard von Rosenberg, knight, assigned to Anna, the wife of his son Engelhard, as part of her dower, the morning gift called — the entire tithe in Boffsheim and ½ of the tithe in Sindolzheim, to be held until redeemed by the aforementioned Engelhard(s) or their heirs for 600 pounds Heller. Additionally, the aforementioned Engelhard assigned other properties to the same Anna — her dower and marriage portion — to be held similarly.

We found the elder Engelhard as a knight in 1361 in an Amorbach document. A document dated 1398, Thursday before Invocavit, pertains to his son. Bishop Gerhard of Würzburg enfeoffed Engelhard von Rosenberg with the properties in the village of Getzikeim, which he purchased from Herolt von Buchein and his heirs.

The same Engelhard von Rosenberg was enfeoffed in 1387 with a farm, including its appurtenances, and a castle estate in Binsfeld. A bit later, he appears repeatedly in close association with an Eberhard von Rosenberg; in 1399, both are witnesses side by side; Mon. Zoll. VI, 55.

In 1406, on the eve of Palm Sunday, Bishop Johannes of Würzburg acknowledges: Engelhard and Eberhard von Rosenberg sold all their fiefs and properties in Gözigheim to Arnold von Rosenberg the Younger for 200 florins, with the right of repurchase, which the feudal lord approved.

In 1411, Engelhard and Eberhard von Rosenberg sold their own people in Uffingen to the Teutonic Order.

We are therefore inclined to consider these two men as brothers, and this assumption is confirmed by a later document from 1444, according to which Ulrich von Rosenberg received those fiefs that had passed down to him from his ancestor Engelhard (I.) and from his father Eberhard. Therefore, Engelhard I. also had a son named Eberhard.

Regarding the possessions of this line, some information is provided by the enfeoffment of Engelhard II in 1401. He received from Würzburg his share in Rosenberg and Boßfheim, the large and ½ and ⅙ of the small tithe there, ¼ of the large and small tithe in Sindolzheim, the purchased properties in Gözigheim, a share of the tithe in Schweinburg, 10 florins in cash as a castle fief at Hohenburg, a property in the city, a farm in Halberstadt, and a share in the court at Bremen, with all appurtenances. Now, while these possessions are mostly family properties near the ancestral seat, where the other lines also held shares, the castle fief at Hohenburg stands out as particularly unique and serves as a guide. We will certainly not go wrong if, because of this castle fief, we attribute the sons of an Eberhard von Rosenberg to be the brothers of Engelhard.

In 1417, Cunz von Rosenberg, the youngest son of Eberhard, received a share of the Rosenberg Castle, as well as in Boßheim and Sindolzheim, property and income in Gezigheim. In 1423, Kunz von Rosenberg the younger, son of Eberhard, received properties in Bremen and ⅓ in Buch. The same Kunz von Rosenberg the younger received in 1443 the half tithe in Boßheim, ⅓ of the tithe in Sindolzheim, interest and properties in Gezigheim, and a castle estate in Hohenburg, as it had come to him from his brother Eberhard. This Eberhard von Rosenberg "the younger" had been enfeoffed with the mentioned properties in 1425. A third brother was Ulrich "the younger," who in 1437 was enfeoffed with ¼ of the tithe in Sindolzheim and a castle estate in Hohenburg, as he had inherited it from his father. In 1444, Ulrich von Rosenberg the younger was enfeoffed with the larger share in Rosenberg, which had come to him from his ancestor Engelhard and from his father Eberhard; in 1450, Ulrich the younger inherited from his brother Conz—a castle estate in Hohenburg Castle and a house in the city. In 1458, he sold ¼ of the village of Boßheim to his cousin, Knight Conrad (of Gezigheim). With the consent of his brother Conrad, he sold ¼ of the tithe in Sindelzheim for 400 florins (Biedermann).

Finally, a fourth brother comes to light; in 1436, the brothers Ulrich and Jörg von Rosenberg purchased a quarter of Borberg from their cousin Arnold; see Stocker's Borberg, p. 19. In 1458, Ulrich von Rosenberg (without any addition) received one half for himself and one half for the sons of his deceased brother Georg—⅓ of the tithe in Burgheim, income in Sindolzheim, ¼ of the castle and ½ of the tithe in Rosenberg, ¼ of the village of Boßheim, a castle estate in Hohenburg, properties, and ¼ of the court in Buch, two hamlets in Ersfeld in Brezigheim, etc. This Ulrich "the younger" had a feud with the cities in 1440, appeared in a Balderzheim document in 1442, and had a dispute with Lord Kraft von Hohenlohe over a stolen horse, Wibel III, 78. In 1449, he had, among other disputes, one concerning the buildings in Assmannstadt; in 1458 and 1464, he was bailiff at Metmühl and made an exchange with the chapter there, Mone, Die Rheinische Zeitschrift IX, 439. In 1460, Junker Ulrich von Rosenberg stood surety in a Schönthal document concerning a farm in Assmannstadt. In 1461, he received from Würzburg his ¼ share in the castle and town of Rosenberg and—as the eldest of the Rosenbergs—the church rights in Rosenberg, Burkheim, Wolsheim, Heimsbach, Boßheim, and Ußigheim. On March 8, 1463, he made a castle peace in Schüpf with Georg von Dottenheim and Horned von Hornberg (Mone l.c. XV, 433.), but also in the same year, 1463, in conjunction with the guardians of his brother Georg's children, he sold one-eighth of the castle of Schüpf. In 1464, Ulrich acquired a share in Mainelfes, which had previously belonged to Conrad von Bubenhofen.

Georg von Rosenberg appears, for example, in 1447 during an alliance on the Jagst against the Horneck; in 1453, he was enfeoffed with ¼ of the castle and town of Bofsberg, especially with the large Kempnate and the house above the chapel. According to the Jahrbuch 1861, p. 397, he died on April 28, 1458.

From his children onward, this branch of the family can be followed without significant difficulties. Turning back to the brothers of Engelhard I, we have not found Hermann mentioned again. However, with Eberhard and Conrad, it is difficult, if not impossible, to distinguish them from their cousins with the same names. Therefore, we will first focus on the other main branch of the family, to establish the secure generations there and to find reference points.

Several Würzburg enfeoffments report on the progenitor Arnold:

1317: Arnoldus de Rosenberg, knight, holds as a Würzburg fief ½ of the village of Büffelskeim with its appurtenances. He receives with the consent of Lutzonis Münch—Hirslande village with its appurtenances and all other fiefs of Lutzonis. Mone IX, 1, p. 58. Wibel 4, 41.

Arnoldus de Rosenberg, knight, and Eberhardus and Wipertus, his fraternal brothers—exchange with the Seeligenthal Monastery two parts of the wine tithe in the village and march of Ruchsheim, which they held as a fief from Count Rupert von Dürne and the Count from the Würzburg Church, for 10 pounds Heller of "Herrngulte" income, commonly called, located near Rosenberg Castle in the village of Sindolzheim. This income is then enfeoffed to the Count and the chapter and received from them in 1321. See Gudeni C. D. III, 738 f.

1323? ... de Rosenberg, knight, receives two parts of the tithe in Schweineburg and in Bremen, ½ of the court, and the advocacy.

1325: Arnoldus de Rosenberg receives the patronage rights in Rosenberg, as well as the patronage rights in Burcheim and an income of 30 solidi there. Additionally, he receives the patronage rights in Boeffelsheim, ½ of the tithe in Schweineburg, and in Bremen, the court and ½ of the advocacy, as well as ½ of the tithe in Syndolzhausen and in Gnetzenkeim, the fief of the said Heiden.

In a sale to the Gerlachsheim Monastery, Lord Arnold von Rosenberg, son-in-law of "des Phales," acted as guarantor for Count Rudolf von Wertheim in 1321 (Mone, D. Rhein IX, p. 58), so we also know the family of his wife. His sons must be the cousins of Eberhard I's sons—Eberhard (II.), Conrad, and Hans—who were already mentioned above; see Reg. b. VII, 298. This is further confirmed by some enfeoffments.

The Lord Bishop of Würzburg granted in 1359, at the request of Eberhard, the son of the late Arnold von Rosenberg, to be passed on to his children—Ludwig, also known as Münch von Rosenberg, knight, and Johann, his son—certain tithes, including the wine tithe in Meckmühl, the rye tithe in Oberzimern, 1/6 of the rye tithe in Burgheim, 1/4 of the rye tithe in Rosenberg, and 1/12 of the same, the estate called Dürrhof there, the estate called Budenhof in Sindolzheim near the cemetery; also 1/4 of the village and the court in Boßsheim.

1366: Eberhard von Rosenberg, son of Arnold, received all the goods that Conrad and Eberhard, brothers from Hartheim, had held as a fief from the Lord Bishop in the village and march of Bretzigheim, through the sale and resignation of the two, with the exception of their vineyards.

1366: Eberhard von Rosenberg received an income of 10 pounds Heller from the estate in Bretzigheim with its appurtenances—inhabited by Itel Ketel there—and the remaining part still held by Ketel, through the sale and resignation of the aforementioned Ketel.

This Eberhard (II), Arnold's son, could very well be the Eberhard referred to with his wife Wyclo, who lived in Zimmern in the 13th century and owned property in Kirchheim; see above. Indeed, I am inclined to think of this Eberhard because his mother, born a Pfahl von Grünsfeld, could quite possibly have passed these possessions on to her children, as Eberhard already had children living in 1359. Eberhard von Rosenberg of Zimmern (and associates) was reconciled with Lord Kraft von Hohenlohe on St. Vincent's Day in 1347 over certain funds and the estate of Zimmern, over which they had fought; see Fleischners Chronik.

In 1379, Peter von Helmstadt, known as von Rosenberg, son of Anna von Mure, residing in Helmstadt, is mentioned, and in 1374, this Peter von Helmstadt, known as von Rosenberg, exchanged properties with his brother Raban, sealed by Kunz Münch von Rosenberg. In 1396, Hans von Helmstadt, known as von Rosenberg, was again residing in Helmstadt (Mone XV, 186), but in a document from 1412, he is again referred to as Hans von Helmstadt, called von Rosenberg

In 1368, Eberhard was co-guardian of the children of his niece Anna von Rosenberg, the widow of Johann Pfahl von Grünsfeld; see Mone, D. Rhein XVIII, 314 f. Whether he is the same Eberhard von Rosenberg who was the brother-in-law of Bertold von Zwingenberg in 1364, we cannot say; see Hanzselmann II, 100. According to a written note, purportedly derived from documents by Pastor Leutwein of Schüpf, the Lords of Rosenberg purchased the castle and town of Boßberg from the Knights of St. John in 1381 for 18,000 florins, specifically Eberhard, Arnold, Conrad, and Eberhard von Rosenberg. The following enfeoffment is certainly confirmed (Mone, D. Rhein IX, p. 437.):

1381, May 25: The Electors Palatine of the Rhine, Ruprecht the Elder and the Younger, acknowledge that their loyal subjects, Eberhard, knight, and Arnold, brothers of Rosenberg, have surrendered to them their share, i.e., half of the Bocksberg Castle, fortress, and town with its appurtenances, as rightful property, and have received it back as a proper fief. They have also made it an open house for the three Ruprechts. In return, the Electors promise to defend the aforementioned Rosenbergs as they would any of their other vassals, and if anyone were to attack Bocksberg Castle, they would faithfully assist, as they would for any other of their own or open castles and houses. Dt. Heidelberg.

Arnold, however, sold his share in Boxberg in 1387 to his brother, as he had acquired a share in Schüpf; both brothers had their rights to the court at Boxberg confirmed by Emperor Wenceslaus in 1388 (Stocker, p. 19). The court at Schweigern was granted by King Wenceslaus in 1397, l. c. p. 55.

Here, we can confidently assume that the children of Eberhard II are meant, particularly because the second brother carries the name of his grandfather, Arnold II. This Arnold sealed documents as Junker A. von Rosenberg, noble servant, in 1380, and was known as Arnolt von Rosenberg "the elder" in 1400 when he served as one of five commissioners for collecting a general Würzburg land tax (Reg. b. XI, 177). Since references to an Arnolt "the younger" appear in 1405 and 1406, it is certain that the older Arnolt lived quite a long time. Indeed, he appears once more on May 1, 1409; Arnolt von Rosenberg, the elder, residing in Schüpf, with his wife Elsbet, sold properties and rents in Ober-Dypach, Niedernhall, and Crispenhofen to the Schönthal Monastery for 26 florins. For this reason, the date in Stocker's Boxberg must be incorrect if it states on page 19 that Arnold the elder's widow guaranteed a loan for Eberhard the younger in 1401 when he transferred 415 florins to his brother Arnold.

We first encounter an Arnold von Rosenberg the Younger, residing in Boxberg, in 1394, as a co-sealer of a document (Reg. b. XI, 3). In 1395, Arnold von Rosenberg the Younger and Eberhard von Rosenberg, brothers and sons of the late Lord Eberhard von Rosenberg, sold a meadow in Kupprichhausen to Martine. The recurrence of the name Arnold here makes it likely that we are dealing with members of the same family branch. We therefore believe that the father, Eberhard, was the Eberhard von Rosenberg, knight, who is referred to as "Vogt of Lauda" in a 1385 document but had already died on June 15, 1388, as recorded in the necrology in the Jahrbuch of 1861: "Eberhardus de R. miles." His grave monument is likely the one in Wölchingen, which Schönhuth perhaps inaccurately describes with the inscription: "In the year 1387, on the Sunday after Bithy (June 15), Lord Effard von Rosenberg passed away." According to a handwritten note, the maternal coat of arms seems to be that of the Lords of Thüngen; see 1856, p. 34.

The brothers Arnolt the Younger and Eberhard from 1395 likely belong to him. King Ruprecht allowed the brothers Arnolt and Eberhard von Rosenberg on December 10, 1400, to have a bailiff and aldermen, a high court, stocks, and gallows in their village of Schweigern. (Chmel 32). The same Roman King Ruprecht issued a document in 1408; since our dear Arnolt von Rosenberg the Younger holds the fortress of Boxberg and all its appurtenances as a fief from our Palatinate, we allow him to exchange the estates of Erlenbach and Merchingen, which belong to the Boxberg share. Following this, Arnolt von Rosenberg the Younger, residing in Bocksberg, and his wife Christine von Henscheseim exchanged their properties in Erlebach, located near Ballenberg, and in Merchingen for Schöntal properties in Buch, in the Gew and Sachsen. The seals are from—Eberhard von Rosenberg, my brother, residing in Jacksberg, the honorable and steadfast knight, and Conrad von Rosenberg, my cousin, residing in Bockberg; 1408, on St. Martin's Day.

In 1406, Arnolt von Rosenberg the Younger purchased properties in Gözigheim from the brothers Engelhard and Eberhard von Rosenberg; see above.

In July 1415, Count Johann von Wertheim approved the sale by his vassal Arnold von Rosenberg (no longer referred to as "junior") of properties in Niccolzhausen (Niclashausen) to the monastery in Grünau. Mone, D. Rhein XVIII, 323. In 1419, Arnold von Rosenberg, with his wife Christine von Hantschuchsheim, sold properties in Gommersdorf, which had previously belonged to the castle at Boxberg, to the Schöntal Monastery for 750 Rhenish florins. In 1436, Arnold sold his quarter share in Boxberg to his cousins Ulrich and Jörg von Rosenberg.

The brother Eberhard has not been mentioned often.

On Tuesday before Corpus Christi Day in 1409, Bishop Johann von Würzburg acknowledges that Eberhard von Rosenberg, residing in Jagsberg, had purchased 2 florins in cash on the farm with its appurtenances and the market at Jagsberg and Mulsingen, which he had bought for the chapel at Jagsberg from Conzen for Golftstein of Galtenhofen for a sum of 30 Rhenish florins, with the right of repurchase (?).

In 1414, Eberhard von Rosenberg of Jagsberg reappears, see Reg. boic. XII, 177.

Two gravestones in Wölchingen seem to belong here, see 1856, p. 34. On St. Walpurgis Day, 1449, Eberhard von Rosenberg died, and on the Thursday after Trinity Sunday in 1447, Arnolt von Rosenberg passed away. Their mother's coat of arms shows a lion, so they are undoubtedly brothers, which aligns with the above. This mother is likely Els von Erleheim (Erligheim?), who, according to Stocker's Boßberg p. 48, was the wife of an Eberhard von Rosenberg and endowed half of her income in Gommersdorf for a Mass in Wölchingen, which was confirmed by their liege lord, Elector Palatine Ruprecht, in 1393.

Eberhard certainly held Jagsberg as a Würzburg fief, as in 1406 Jagsberg completely passed into episcopal possession. This fief was likely redeemed soon after 1414, as a bit later the Lords of Hornberg held Jagsberg, which was taken from them in 1437 after a siege. The former Jagsberger is likely the same Eberhard von Rosenberg who reconciled Otto Platz with the city of Hall in 1421; who served as a witness in 1427, see Reg. b. XIII, 110; and who exchanged a stable at the castle in Bocksberg with his cousins Ulrich and Jörg in 1447 (Stoder). He is likely the same Eberhard who, in 1449, was once again mentioned as the late, honorable Eberhard von Rosenberg, as he was involved in the aforementioned dispute with the peasants of Asmanstadt (near Bocksberg).

It remains uncertain which Eberhard von Rosenberg purchased properties and rents in Gissigheim from Konz von Buchheim in 1398.

Biedermann, in Table 405, claims with certainty that a Michael von Rosenberg of Schüpff appeared in 1407, 08, and 11. Since we have at least found one Michael von Rosenberg, who in 1378 sold his half tithe in Bobstadt to the Teutonic Order for 140 florins, we will tentatively identify this Michael as another son of Eberhard, a brother of Arnold I.

I now turn back to Hans (not Heinz) von Rosenberg, Arnold's son, who was mentioned in 1341 along with his brothers Eberhard and Conrad and in the same year sealed the document (likely of his brother) Eberhard von Rosenberg with his wife Wychlo, Reg. b. VII, 306 f., as Joannes de Rosenberg. In 1346, Hans von Rosenberg stood as guarantor for the Bishop of Würzburg, see Reg. b. VIII, 81; in 1355, he is referred to in an Amorbach document as Johans von Rosenberg, known as von Neydeck (Oberamt-Description of Dehringen); in 1361, he was a knight and became a Burgmann in Mergentheim, during which he transferred to the Teutonic Order the ownership of a farm in Berolzheim in exchange for 100 heller. In 1363, Hans von Rosenberg, knight, was a guarantor for Mainz, see Reg. b. IX, 81; and in 1369—Johann and Hans—l.c. IX, 224, 227. According to a Würzburg fief book, Joannes de Rosenberg was enfeoffed with the castrum Truhusen along with its appurtenances, which he had purchased from Gutend von Uffenheim (likely a Lord of Seckendorf?); he and Arnold von Thalheim gave it up.

We receive news of children in 1368. Sir Johann von Rosenberg, knight, had a daughter, Anna, who had been married to Knight Johann Pfol von Grünsfeld and, as a widow in February 1368, with the involvement of the guardians of her children, the noblemen Eberhard von Rosenberg and Heinrich Pfol, sold the large tithe in Großrinderfeld; see Mone, D. Rhein XVIII, 314 f. Her own dowry had been assigned to the village of Kirchheim, l.c. p. 318.

Brothers of Anna appear later; in 1387, Götz von Rosenberg and his brothers (or brother?) received their share in Rosenberg, a castle fief in Rodenfels, and properties in Esselbach. The father was likely deceased by then. In 1385, Hans von Rosenberg & his wife Anna von Kronenberg and his brother Götz sold their fortress in Urhausen, as it had been inherited from their late father, for 215 florins. The guarantors were: Knight Eberhard von Rosenberg, Vogt of Lauda, and Conz von Rosenberg, residing in Boxberg.

In 1401, Hans von Rosenberg received from Würzburg his share of the large and small tithes in Brunnacker and Burkheim in Boßfheim, what he had there, his share of the house in Rosenberg, and a castle fief belonging to Rodenfels.

In 1401, Götz von Rosenberg received his share in Rosenberg, as well as his share of the tithe in Rosenberg, Brunnacker, and Burkheim; likewise, in Boßfheim, what he held there, and a castle fief belonging to Rodenfels.

The two brothers, Hans and Götz, transferred 20 malters of grain rent from the tithe in Burkheim to Arnold von Rosenberg the Younger, with whom Arnold was enfeoffed in 1405.

Hans received 60 florins from the burgrave in 1402 and 150 florins in 1406, see Reg. b. XI, 276, 380.

In 1412, Götz von Rosenberg received from Würzburg as a fief his share of properties and tithes in Rosenberg, his share of the tithe in Burkheim, and in the village of Boßfheim and the new farm near Rosenberg. Hans von Rosenberg received ⅛ of the tithe in Burgheim, 2 parts of ¼ of the tithe in Rosenberg, his and his brother's house in Rosenberg, as far as the fortified courtyard reached, and 5 properties—his share in Boßfheim. Götz von Rosenberg received the tithe in Boßfheim, ⅓ of the tithe in Sindolzheim, and other properties.

Shortly thereafter, two brothers, Hans the Younger and Thomas, appear, enfeoffed in 1415, and in 1421 Cunz von Rosenberg, the son of Hans von Rosenberg. It is evident that Götz had passed away, and he was succeeded by the two brothers, Thomas and Hans, who was first called "the Younger" because his uncle Hans was still alive for some time. Hans von Rosenberg the Younger received in 1415 the tithe in Burkheim, his share of the tithe in Rosenberg, his share of the village of Boßfheim with the court and its appurtenances, and the income in Sindolzheim, which his brother Thomas von Rosenberg had previously held as a fief and now relinquished.

Cunz von Rosenberg, son of Hans von Rosenberg, received in 1421 a ¼ share of the tithe in Burkheim, properties in Boßfheim and Espelbach*), and a stone house in Rosenberg. The three mentioned brothers and cousins sealed a deed of sale in 1425 concerning a share in Rosenberg Castle: (Cunz von Rosenberg of Bartenstein,) Thomas and Hans von Rosenberg, brothers, Cunz von Rosenberg the Younger—

*) Where in 1480, Fritz von Dürrn mortgaged his life; Rhön u. Werra T. 881.

Thomas von Rosenberg was in a feud with the city of Heilbronn from 1426 to 1429; see Jäger's Geschichte von Heilbronn I, 186 f. In 1429, the city demanded that he sever ties with Eberhard the Younger von Doltenheim, who had become their enemy. Therefore, Thomas likely resided in Schüpf.

In 1426, Thomas von Rosenberg acted as a guarantor in a Gnadenthal document, Wibel II, 217. In 1429, he was enfeoffed with ½ of the tithe in Rosenberg and ⅛ of the tithe in Burkheim, inherited from his father; in 1437, Konrad von Weinsberg purchased 100 malters of oats from him at the Mergentheim fair for 100 florins.

According to the necrology of the Dominican Monastery in Mergentheim, Sir Thomas, knight of Rosenberg, died on January 24, 1458. We know as little about his descendants as we do about the further fate of his brother Hans. However, in the Würzburg fief books of 1445 and 1456, a Hans von Rosenberg "the Younger" appears and receives ⅓ of the tithe in Burkheim and his share in the village of Boßheim, in Rosenberg Castle, and in the village of Eßelbach or Espelbach, inherited from his father Cunz von Rosenberg, who is naturally the son of the elder Hans (from 1421), see above.

Mysteriously, an Eberhard von Rosenberg also reappears in 1452, who, alongside Ulrich, was enfeoffed with ¼ of Borberg, but sold his ¼ share to Ulrich in the same year with the consent of Sir Thomas von Rosenberg, knight, and Jörgen von Rosenberg (who is Ulrich's brother and co-owner of Borberg). This Eberhard, who sold his share in Borberg with Thomas's approval, can only be thought of as a member of the Hansian line, perhaps as a brother of Hans V. This line likely came into possession of a share in Borberg as an inheritance after the death of their cousins Arnold III and Eberhard VI. We have not yet encountered this Eberhard VIII elsewhere, unless he is the knight Eberhard von Rosenberg who allegedly, in 1488 with his wife Ursula von Adelsheim, established a Mass in Crailsheim, with the wife also donating an income in Wolmershausen to the St. John's Church in Crailsheim.

In 1460, a Junker Hans von Rosenberg is mentioned in Heilbronn documents as a judge and councilor in Heilbronn. According to Schannat's Clientela Fuldensis, p. 79, Johann von Rosenberg purchased a fief, commonly called a "Reitlehen," and a remnant in Lengsfeld in 1460. Is this always the same Hans V.? We do not know of a Hans senior in 1445 and 1456, unless Hans IV lived that long without appearing in documents.

As noted above, Hans was enfeoffed again in 1468 and once more in 1485, but these two times without the designation "the younger." In 1489, he is referred to as Hans von Rosenberg of Ußingen and was a Palatinate court judge. Along with his cousin Conrad von Rosenberg, he had a dispute with the Schönthal Monastery in 1487 over the tithe in Hirschlanden. He died shortly thereafter, and in 1491, Hans von Rosenberg's sons were enfeoffed: Hans and Georg, who intended to become a cleric. Hans alone received his Würzburg fiefs again in 1497 and 1519; in 1494, he acted as a guarantor for Wendel von Adelsheim; in 1521, Hans von Rosenberg sold ⅓ of the large and small tithe, a farm, and a mill in Sindringen to Schönthal; see Schönhuth's Schönthal, p. 139. In 1525, he was killed in the Peasants' War, and his son Christoffel failed to receive the fiefs, which therefore lapsed. Christoffel later requested the bishop to transfer the fiefs at least to his son Hans Gucharius, and due to the willing service this son had rendered to the chapter for some time, he was re-enfeoffed in 1536. Christoffel von Rosenberg was recommended by the Palatinate elector to the King of France for service in 1497; see Ludewig, Reliq. mscr. VI, 97.

Hans Cargus in 1542, or Hans Cargus in 1544, and Hans Eucharius in 1558, were again enfeoffed with ⅓ of the tithe in Burkheim, ⅛ in Rosenberg, the Tief Valley, and the income in Obertief, and the house in Rotenstal, and Westheim, among others, as Seitz Häuptlein had previously held. Shortly thereafter, he seems to have died, while his widow endowed the St. Michael's Chapel in Rotenburg in 1449. See Mittelspäntisches Archiv 36, 72.

Since we have successfully placed all the other Conrads, the brother of Knight Hans must be the Conrad von Rosenberg who was enfeoffed with the fortress of Bartenstein by the Lords of Hohenlohe in 1421 and 1432, as he had purchased it from Hans von Seldenet the Elder.

In 1423, Kunz von Rosenberg of Bartenstein appears as a guarantor (alongside C. von Rosenberg of Reichelsberg); see 1857, p. 224; likewise, in 1425, in a Mergentheim document (alongside Kunz von Rosenberg the Younger, i.e., the son of the Reichelsberger).

Which of these two was enfeoffed in 1432 with a house and courtyard in Röttingen, purchased from Fritz von Giebelheim by the Lords of Hohenlohe, we must leave undecided. Kunz von Rosenberg "of Bartenstein" is explicitly mentioned again in 1435 at a Würzburg fief court (the above-cited Hohenlohe deduction against Würzburg, p. 29 of the appendices). In 1436, he received from the bishop, as he held no other fief from the chapter, for his good services, the toll at the half-village of Rippach (Niedbach), and if he were to die without sons, this fief should be redeemed for his daughters with 2000 florins. In 1438, among the hostages for Bishop Johann of Würzburg was C. von Rosenberg of Bartenstein. Therefore, the account in the Oberamt-Description of Gerabronn, p. 116, may be misleading, as it suggests that the Horneck of Hornberg were already co-owners at that time "as successors to Conrad von Rosenberg." The change of ownership could not have occurred until shortly before the capture of Bartenstein on November 21, 1438, due to the robberies committed by the co-owners von Seldeneck. In the settlement, the Horneck were compensated for their share of the castle and the village of Riedbach with 3000 florins in gold, and with 1100 florins in gold for the pledged share of the toll there.

Kunz indeed seems to have only had daughters, and his line disappears with him. Whether he became a co-founder of the chapel at Maienfels as "Gauerbe" in 1433 (see Oberamt-Description of Weinsberg, p. 291) remains unknown. Since he is not referred to as "von Bartenstein" and Conrad VI operated in a more distant region, we would prefer to consider the Maienfels Conrad as the lesser-known Kunz VII.

The remaining question now is where to place the two progenitors of these Conrad lines in the family tree? Considering everything, the times and other circumstances seem to fit best if we consider Lord Conrad of the Amberg bishopric to be the son of Arnold, and the longer-living Heidelberg bishopric Conrad to be the son of Eberhard I; this arrangement aligns all the generations with the parallel generations of the other lines. In this manner, we will now draft a family tree that includes only the documented individuals, leaving out the many names that Biedermann derived from the tournament books. We do not deny that some actual individuals may not have appeared in the records; others may still incorporate them. We are satisfied if a solid foundation for further genealogical research is established, something that has been lacking until now.

We have already learned about the progenitor of the Boxberg-Schüpfer main line, Jörg I von Rosenberg, and seen that he died as a nobleman in 1458. His sons were minors, as evidenced by the fact that in 1463, Heinrich Zobel, Hans von Absberg, and Ulrich von Rosenberg, acting as guardians of the children of Georg von Rosenberg and Ulrich von Rosenberg, simultaneously sold, in his name, to Georg von Dottenheim, 1/8 of Schüpf Castle, which had previously belonged to Anselm von Rosenberg, who had acquired it from Eberhard von Dottenheim, for 1000 Rhenish florins.

By 1468, they had come of age, and the three brothers Michael, Georg, and Arnoldt von Rosenberg renewed the castle peace for Schüpf with the Lords of Dottenheim; see Mone's Oberrhein XVI, p. 433 ff. No more than four Lords of Rosenberg were entitled to be co-owners of their half of Schüpf, with each quarter of this half—thus 1/8 of the whole—valued at 1200 florins. In 1469, the three brothers participated in the endowment of a benefice in the chapel at Sachsenflur; Wibel III, 167, Jahresheft 1856, 57.

The brothers were belligerent lords and took care to fortify their residence securely, along with the town of Boxberg. To better defend it, they built walls up to the castle and excavated a lake in the plain, while in the castle itself, they dug a well 48 fathoms deep into the rock. In 1469, Georg had a feud with the city of Hall, during which (as Herold recounts) he secretly entered the city and, among other things, burned down the village of Wolpertshausen (D. A. Hall p. 319) and roamed far and wide through the area (Jägers Geschichte von Heilbronn I, 237). As a result, the Rosenbergs gained a bad reputation as robbers and oppressors, and when Georg finally captured Weiprecht Sützel in a somewhat dubious manner, a severe storm began to brew against them.

Archbishop Adolf of Mainz, Elector Friedrich of the Palatinate, and Bishop Rudolf of Würzburg concluded an agreement on the Monday before St. Anthony's Day in 1470 to use their combined forces (each contributing 200 cavalry and 300 infantry) to first capture the fortified churchyards in Schweigern and Schüpf and then, if no agreement was reached in the meantime, to conquer the castles of Boxberg and Schüpf. This plan was carried out. After a heavy bombardment lasting twelve days, Georg fled from Boxberg with 70 cavalry, and the three allies then took possession and established a castle peace among themselves. The castle in Oberschüpf was destroyed and was not rebuilt.

After long negotiations, the Lords of Rosenberg, mainly through the mediation of Margrave Albrecht of Ansbach and the Bishop of Bamberg, regained Boxberg in 1477. However, they had to promise to receive their "castle," if they were to restore it, as a fief from the three allied princes. Georg von Rosenberg, with the help of his wife Johanna von Eicholzheim, zealously undertook the restoration from 1480 to 1493, though they did not refer to it as a castle due to the terms of the agreement, but rather as a bastion. Georg (also known for a knightly duel with Sigmund von Stetten) soon resumed his old ways and engaged in a feud, particularly in support of his cousin, the daughter of Anselm von Rosenberg (see above). This led to a conflict with the Bishop of Würzburg in 1486, who was ready to march against Boxberg with significant force. However, friendly mediators managed to negotiate an agreement in 1487, in which the Bishop paid 5700 florins, although he estimated his damages at 80,000 florins. See Stocker's Boxberg p. 20 ff. Jahresheft 1856.

In 1480, Jörg von Rosenberg received permission to take a loan of 400 florins on his Würzburg fiefs in Rosenberg (Biedermann, Table 406, A.). In 1488, he mediated a dispute between Lord Philipp the Elder of Weinsberg and the Truchsesses of Baldersheim and others concerning Aub. He was a member of the Society of the Unicorn (Unterfränkisches Archiv XIX, 2, p. 196) and participated in several tournaments of that time. In 1500, he was in a feud with Count Asmus von Wertheim. In 1502, he supported Margrave Casimir against Nuremberg and fought on the Palatine side in the Bavarian Succession War in 1504. In 1509, Osanna, née von Eicholzheim, was referred to as the widow of Lord Georg von Rosenberg of Boxberg, knight; they had no children.

Arnold, the brother, appears several times in Baldersheim documents, such as in 1478, and was also a member of the Society of the Unicorn. He participated in the feud against Würzburg in 1487; in 1498, he made an agreement with Conrad von Dottenheim regarding the castle of Schüpf, dated in Freiburg im Breisgau. In 1509, he received his Würzburg fiefs, namely ¼ of the castle and town of Rosenberg; as the eldest of the family, he also received the church rights and ¼ of the village of Boßheim. In 1512, he assigned the gentlemen Zeißolf and Leonhard von Rosenberg as his guarantors against Hans von Rosenberg for indemnification of 700 florins on his share of Rosenberg, which he had also once mortgaged to the dean of Mosbach for 1000 florins; see Biedermann, Table 406, B. His wife is named as Anna von Dottenheim, daughter of Conrad von Dottenheim, the last of that family. Arnold certainly left behind four sons: Hans Thomas (who had assisted Sickingen against Worms in 1516), Hans Melchior, Hans Ulrich, and Hans Kaspar. These sons were reconciled with their cousins in Boxberg in 1518 after intense disputes regarding the joint ownership of Boxberg. However, the first three became entangled in the affairs of Thomas von Absberg; the army of the Swabian League, led by Georg Truchsess von Waldburg, therefore marched against Boxberg, which the Lords of Rosenberg abandoned with their cavalry. The castle yielded rich spoils; it was burned down and handed over to the Elector of the Palatinate as the feudal heir in exchange for war compensation of 5000 florins; see Stocker's Boxberg p. 26 ff.

Hans Thomas exacted his revenge on the Truchsess by secretly kidnapping his son, whom he held captive for a long time in a Franconian castle until, after his father's death, the guardians released him for a ransom of 8000 gold florins.

Schüpf was spared due to the other co-owners; however, Boxberg had not only belonged to Arnold's sons but also to Michael's sons, which naturally led to new complications with the Palatinate.

The four sons of Arnold seem to have had no descendants, as we know nothing further about them; it is likely that the report refers to them that the Rosenbergers turned to Duke Ulrich of Württemberg—the exiled—after the destruction of their castle.

Georg I's son, Michael, who was not yet of age in 1459, appears several times in Baldersheim documents, such as in 1479 and 1496; he was also a member of the Society of the Unicorn. In 1481, Michel mortgaged his village of Schweigern, along with the Seehof, for 2000 florins to the Palatinate; 1856, p. 39. In 1484, the three brothers issued a promissory note for 1000 florins at 5%, and Michael exchanged the chapel garden at Boxberg with his brothers for a homestead; Stocker p. 19. On June 8, 1489, Johann Landgrave of Leuchtenberg enfeoffed Michael von Rosenberg of Boxberg with the estate of Mepstetten (Impfingen a. T.) and the wine tithe in Marbach, as both had come to him by grace from our dear ancestor, Count Philip of Rhineg; however, it was to remain harmless to us and the Lordship of Grünsfeld. Mone IX, p. 316 f.

Michael's wife was a Rüd, and he himself died in 1508. For in that year, the Bishop of Würzburg enfeoffed Georg von Rosenberg, the late Michael's son, with 1/8 of all the tithe in Oberlauda, as his father had held it. The tithe had previously been a Palatinate fief.

In 1516, Georg von Rosenberg of Boxberg acknowledged a debt of 100 florins to the parish benefice at Jagsthausen; signed by his brother Hans; see 1859, p. 35. A third brother was named Eberhard (VIII).

Before we proceed to the next generation, it should be noted that a Friedrich von Rosenberg appears several times, who also likely belonged to the Boxberg line. In 1491, Friedrich von Rosenberg of Boxberg sealed a document from Königshofen; see 1856, p. 62. In 1494, Friedrich von Rosenberg issued a reversal to the Palatinate regarding the redemption of the tithe in Schweigern, which had been pledged for 1000 florins; Stocker, p. 55. Finally, according to the document shared in 1867, p. 599, Friedrich von Rosenberg was the heir of Jörg II and, instead of paying the 1240 florins with which he was supposed to compensate the heirs of Jörg's wife, the Eicholzheimer siblings, he gave his share of the village of Schillingstat—on the condition of redemption. We can only reconcile all this by assuming that Friedrich was an otherwise unknown additional brother of Jörg, who inherited Jörg's allodial property, while the other brothers and their descendants inherited the fiefs.

Now we proceed to Michael's sons. As early as 1513, Georg and Hans von Rosenberg, brothers, and Heinz Rüd von Bödigheim, as curator of their brother Eberhard, had pledged their village of Hohenstat to the Bishop of Würzburg as a fief, from which in 1513, Georg received one half, and in 1520, Hans received the other half. In 1518, Elector Palatine Friedrich and Margrave Kasimir mediated an agreement for Hans Thoma von Rosenberg and his brothers in the disputes between them and Georg von Rosenberg, along with his brothers and cousins. The former were to reside in Boxberg again as before the expulsion with the town, rents, and appurtenances, as they had been before, and to share a castle peace with the latter. *) This likely refers to the destruction in 1470. Since Arnold did not assist in the reconstruction, it seems the cousins did not want his descendants to rejoin as co-owners.

In 1525, Georg von Rosenberg was among the defenders of the Würzburg Castle Frauenberg against the peasants.

Georg III seems to have predominantly resided in the small castle at Schweigern. In 1516, he reached an agreement with the community that they should pay him 140 florins annually instead of the owed so-called Adelsheim feudal labor and dues. In 1521, he renewed the village ordinances; in 1524, he granted the Electoral Palatinate extensive rights of requisition in his castle at Schweigern; in 1525, he mortgaged his half of the village to the Palatinate and promised to redeem the 700 florins borrowed against the tithe; in 1526, he assigned an additional 70 florins to the Palatinate on his share; and finally, in 1535, Elector Ludwig granted Georg von Rosenberg his castle Winzingen (?) on the condition of redemption, with the promise not to redeem it during the lifetime of Georg, his wife, and his son Michael. In return, Georg handed over his castle and half of the village of Schweigern, along with the Seehof and the small village of Windisch-Buch, as payment to the Elector; see Stocker's Boxberg p. 56. With this, it appears that Georg left our region.

His son, Michael II, is said to have appeared in 1541, and Biedermann also attributes a son named Georg to him; Table 404, A. However, it is more likely that it was Georg III who, in November 1540, renounced the burgrave's claim regarding Sachsen; see the deluxe edition of Götz von Berlichingen p. 293. According to a registration note, Elector Ludwig in 1540 mortgaged 700 florins to Jörg von Rosenberg against the pledge of ½ of the wine tithe in Schweigern. According to Würzburg fief notes, in 1553, Albrecht von Rosenberg of Boxberg received the entire village of Hohenstat, of which he had previously owned half, and the other half now fell to him through the death of Michael von Rosenberg. This confirms that Michael II died at that time without surviving heirs to the fiefs.

The brother Hans is rarely mentioned in documents; in 1513, he was of age, and by 1537, he had died, as his nephew Albrecht received half of the village of Hohenstat that year, which had come to him from his cousin Hans. This also proves his death without heirs to the fiefs; other properties were inherited by his brother Georg III.

We should not forget a sister, Anna von Rosenberg, who was married first to Kaspar von Stetten zu Kocherstetten, † 1514, and second to Wolf von Stetten † 1547; Anna herself died in 1548 and is buried in Kocherstetten.

The remaining brother, Eberhard, was not yet of age in 1517 (see above) and died on St. Gall's Day in 1519 in Stuttgart, leaving behind a young widow, Anna née von Dinem (Dienheim), who died on November 26, 1568. Their son Albrecht erected a joint grave monument for both parents, which is of genealogical significance due to the eight ancestral coats of arms. According to these, the father's wife was a Rüd; the grandfather Georg I's wife was a von Kronenberg; the great-grandfather Eberhard V's wife was a von Handschuhzheim. These ancestors are repeatedly found on Albrecht's grave monument, and the Rüd and Kronenberg coats of arms are also indicated on Anna's tombstone in Kocherstetten.

We now feel confident in interpreting the nameless monument in Wölchingen, where a Lord von Rosenberg, in armor, kneels opposite his wife; the lion at his feet holds the coat of arms of the Lords of Handschuhsheim. This is certainly Eberhard V with his wife, who died in 1437. Indeed, the monument belongs to the 15th century, judging by the costume. The note is too vague: in 1521, the Lords of Rosenberg sold properties, revenues, and their own people in Heßfeld, Lauda, Marbach, and Königshofen on the Tauber to the Bishop of Würzburg.

We first found a note about Eberhard's son Albrecht in 1526, regarding his consent (likely that of the guardians) to provide for his mother in Unterschüpf and to grant her a morning gift. In 1534, he first received his Hohenlohe fiefs—two parts of the tithe in Neidlingen and Dach, along with a farm in Horrenbach—and again in 1553, 1555, 1561, 1562, and 1570.

Albrecht had inherited his father's legal claims to a share, at least, of Boxberg. Despite the fact that the Swabian League had not only destroyed the castle because Arnold von Rosenberg's sons had assisted Thomas von Absberg, but had also sold the entire lordship to the Palatinate, an overwhelmingly powerful neighbor, Albrecht energetically pursued his claims in two directions. Against the Palatinate, he took the legal route and sued for restitution, based on the sales contract in which the Palatinate had committed itself to represent the League. At that time, the members of the former Swabian League were negotiating the renewal of the League, and Albrecht appealed to them as well; the League assembly in Augsburg promised to mediate with the Palatinate, but without success.

Albrecht von Rosenberg then declared to the cities of the League on December 21, 1536, that since the Elector Palatine had continually postponed and offered excuses, he was now turning to them again, requesting their assistance in compensating him for his damages, failing which he would take action against them and their allies with fire and plunder. Endless negotiations followed; individual cities, such as Heilbronn, which was closest to and therefore most threatened by Rosenberg, were inclined to make peace with him, at least for their part. However, Albrecht's patience ran out, and as a bold warrior, he took justice into his own hands in the old feudal manner. He ambushed and captured a respected councilor, Hieronymus Baumgärtner, who was returning home from the Reichstag in Speyer in 1544, unconcerned and under the protection of an imperial safe-conduct letter, between Sinsheim and Wimpfen (in a forest near Treschklingen). The captive was moved between various castles and fortresses, as Albrecht intended to force the Nurembergers to assist him in regaining possession of Bocksberg and to compensate him for all the lost revenues and so forth.

Nuremberg made diligent efforts to secure the release of its councilor, and in 1545, it seemed that a settlement might finally be reached. However, Albrecht's demands were too great for the cities, and they preferred to prepare themselves to defend against his attacks. The cities' cavalry scoured the land for Rosenberg's forces, and Nuremberg's scouts sought the hidden and frequently changed location where Baumgärtner was being held. On one occasion, an army of 600 men with four field pieces came to Haldenbergstetten, where, in their frustration at finding nothing, they vented their anger on the castle. Nuremberg also appealed to the Emperor, who issued an order for the prisoner to be delivered under threat of outlawry, and various great lords took on the role of mediators, which led to Albrecht himself being granted safe conduct to the Reichstag in Worms—and indeed, the imperial justice system was so prompt that Nuremberg had to pay 10,000 gold gulden to ransom both Baumgärtner and Christoph Kreter, who had also been captured in the meantime\! 1545.

Such self-help was not feasible against the Palatinate.

However, Albrecht had become a very capable officer and had earned the Emperor's favor, particularly during the Schmalkaldic War, while the Elector of the Palatinate had fallen out of favor. As a result, an imperial military force took possession of Boxberg and handed over the town and castle to the plaintiff, Albrecht, in 1547. Albrecht immediately began to rebuild the castle, which had been in ruins since 1523, as attested by a stone inscription in the wall for many years.

The Elector Palatine then filed a lawsuit; however, Albrecht, the brave cavalry colonel who around this time also received the title of knight, had the opportunity to render a very significant personal service to the Emperor. When Charles V was forced to flee quickly from Tyrol after being ambushed by Elector Maurice of Saxony in 1552, Rosenberg accompanied and protected him, and it is said that the Emperor wore Rosenberg's colors at the time, as if he were his squire.

Having safely arrived in the Netherlands, the Emperor granted him an annual pension of 400 florins from the General Receivership of the Netherlands. According to another handwritten note, Albrecht also seized the quarter of Boxberg, to which the Palatinate undoubtedly had legitimate claims (the share of the outlawed Hans Thomas, among others).

Occasionally, I would like to attempt to interpret a local curiosity from Schüpf, see 1856, p. 54.

In a wall of the castle barn, a Rosenberg coat of arms is embedded, on which sits a monkey, with the inscription: I am called a Monkey I am a good boy. 1561.

This coat of arms stone was previously placed on a now-demolished tower, which contained a prison for "bad boys," and it is said that because of the image, the tower was called "the Monkey." This interpretation is clearly very contrived; the people seem to have developed this story when the tower served as a prison, but the inscription itself does not point to this. However, considering that in Götz von Berlichingen's younger years there was a traveling servant known as "the Monkey," see 1858, p. 387, the possibility arises: Lord Albrecht von Rosenberg also had a traveling servant, a knightly servant of this family or at least of this name, who belonged to the castle garrison in Schüpf and to whom that tower was assigned as a residence. Therefore, the emblems of the lord and his servant—the Rosenberg coat of arms and a monkey—were placed above the door. Whether the name "Aff" (Monkey) was used around 1560 in Schüpf could perhaps still be investigated.

Emperor Ferdinand himself also took an interest in the entire dispute and in 1553 brought about a settlement with the cities of the former Swabian League in Augsburg, in which these cities paid a little over 41,000 florins for all the damages Albrecht had suffered. However, the lawsuit with the Palatinate continued, and Albrecht was deprived of Boxberg, leading him to build a modern castle—still standing today—in Unterschüpf, and in 1561 he sold his share in Boxberg to the Elector for 2,400 florins and the transfer of the cent to Schillingstadt, Sachsenflur, Epplingen, and Dainbach, which belonged to the Lordship of Schüpf. Albrecht was also enfeoffed with half of Bobstadt (Stocker).

However, the neighboring disputes were not yet fully resolved; in 1562 and 1564, further agreements were made with the Palatinate regarding various conflicts. The Lordship of Schüpf had previously only partially belonged to the Rosenbergs, with the other half belonging to the Dottenheim family. When the Dottenheim family died out around 1550, various claimants emerged, either by maternal or uxorial right. However, Kurmainz, the feudal lord, initially sequestered the disputed property until the matter could be settled and then, in 1552, enfeoffed the Court Marshal Eberhard Rüd von Collenberg, who also received the small castle in Sachsenflur from the Limburg Schenten:

1561, Tuesday after Invocavit Sunday: Schent Christof von Limburg enfeoffed Eberhard Rüd von Collenberg with the Kemenate in Sachsenflur, along with its courtyard, barns, and gardens, as well as a farm in Sachsenflur, including a lake and a meadow—just as it had previously been held as a fief by David Truchseß and his sons Joachim and Philipp—as both a male and female fief.

This Lord von Rüd sold his Mainz fief, along with the Limburg property, in 1561 to our Albrecht von Rosenberg, who thereby became the sole lord of Schüpf and also acquired the parish fief from the Lords Counts of Hohenlohe. He similarly owned Buch am Ahorn in 1560; in 1559, he bought ¼ of the tithe in Epplingen from two Dottenheim daughters; in 1561, a farm in Sachsenflur, a Würzburg fief.

The following excerpt from the fief book provides the necessary information about the Hohenlohe fiefs:

1555: Albrecht von Rosenberg received ½ of the wine tithe in Oberschüpf, which he had bought from Albrecht von Biberehrn, as well as (a knightly fief in) 1561 and 1562.

1561: Albrecht von Rosenberg, knight, received 35 Malters of grain and other incomes from a farm in Klepsheim, which had once belonged to Dietrich von Rosenberg; also, the right of presentation to the parish of Unterschüpf.

*) Could this still refer to the old Diether we found in 1343? See above.

So also in 1562, and in addition, two Huben (farms) and other incomes in Ahlringen, Unter- and Oberschüpf, as well as the patronage rights of the morning masses in Unterschüpf and Schwaigern; similarly in 1570.

Regarding the Limburg fief, a fief letter was issued on October 15, 1561, by Schent Christof for Albrecht von Rosenberg of Niedernschüpf, knight, who had purchased the Sachsenflur fief from Eberhard Rüd.

The possession of Schüpf soon led to conflicts with Kurmainz, which also held fiefdoms in the surrounding area, leading to a contract in 1565 to settle the mutual rights and claims; see 1856, p. 45.

Knight Albrecht, formerly "of Bocksberg," later "of Niederschüpf," appears as a witness, arbitrator, mediator, guarantor, and in other roles in various documents. He even had a dispute with his cousin Zeisolf in Haldenbergstetten, who still demanded 8,000 florins due to the damage suffered during the Nuremberg invasion, a matter that Albrecht had been obligated to settle in his agreement with the cities. However, this issue had not been resolved by the time of Albrecht's death.

In 1557, Albrecht led the soldiers of the Franconian Circle as commander against the Turks in Hungary; in 1562, he accompanied Duke Christoph of Württemberg to Frankfurt for the coronation of Emperor Ferdinand. Albrecht von Rosenberg was particularly well-suited to Duke Christoph because he had since become a very fervent Lutheran; indeed, he also acquired additional patronage rights from Hohenlohe. In 1563, Lord Albrecht von Rosenberg, knight, received the patronage of the parishes of Oberbalbach and Dettelsingen, as well as other properties and incomes in Ober- and Unterbalbach and Oberlauda. (A. von Rosenberg, in return, made his 1/4 share of Dettelsingen a Hohenlohe fief.)

In 1558, Albrecht requested the evangelical Lutheran preacher and pastor Conrad Hochmuth from the Imperial City of Rothenburg to serve at Boxberg. When he moved to Schüpf, he took this cherished clergyman with him and gradually staffed all his parishes with Lutherans. In contrast, the Palatinate had turned to the Reformed Confession and now appointed Reformed clergy in Boxberg and Schweigern, against which Albrecht directed his subjects to attend his churches; for example, he directed the congregation of Epplingen, previously a filial of Schweigern, to Ußingen. In Schüpf, he established a chaplaincy (diaconate) in 1564 (Wibel I, 413 f.) from the income of the dissolved morning masses and assigned it to Epplingen and Dainbach, under protest from the Palatinate (which in 1681 established its own parish in Dainbach). See Stocker p. 78. In 1562, Albrecht organized a colloquium and examination for his clergy, to which Gallus Hartmann of Dehlingen was invited; Wibel I, 386. III, 183. The entire church administration was to be put in proper order.

Knight Albrecht also turned his attention to civil matters; already in Boxberg, he had established the city's rights, regulated boundary rights, determined burdens and taxes, and organized court procedures, among other things; see the Boxberg city book of 1561.

Similarly, Albrecht organized affairs in Schüpf, issuing a court ordinance, a marriage and school ordinance for his lordship, and acquired market rights and a coat of arms for Unterschüpf; he granted the Ohmgeld (a local tax) to the community. He negotiated with Kurmainz regarding the Centgerichtsbarkeit (jurisdiction over certain criminal cases), which the court in Königshofen claimed; however, his two castles in Unterschüpf and Sachsenflur were graciously exempted.

As a consequence of his travels, Ober- and Unterschüpf, Kupprichhausen, Lengriethen, Uffingen, half of Sachsenflur, and a quarter of Dainbach were connected (this likely constituted the core of the old Lordship of Schüpf).

In his later years, Albrecht became involved in the Grumbach feuds and was declared an outlaw as an accomplice of Grumbach. He was even taken prisoner and brought to Vienna. This misfortune may be related to a loan of 5,300 florins in 1571, despite his otherwise prosperous status.

However, death freed him from all his troubles, as his monument in the Schüpf church attests: he passed away on May 17, 1572, after his wife, Rufina née Stiebar von Buttenheim, had already died on August 26, 1569. Biedermann also mentions a first wife, Katharina von Seinsheim; however, we believe that the gravestone would hardly remain silent about her if this were the case. In any event, Albrecht could not have left behind a son of the same name who allegedly died in 1575, especially since his young son had predeceased him. For, immediately after Albrecht's death in 1572, numerous inheritance disputes arose, with the allodial heirs on one side and the cousins from Rosenberg in Haldenbergstetten, as heirs to the fiefs, on the other. Information about Albrecht's assets was once provided by the following records:

1571/92: Negotiations of the allodial heirs of Albrecht von Rosenberg with the Royal Bohemian Chamber against Lady Häßin von Hasenberg regarding the return of the valuable movable property deposited by Albrecht von Rosenberg in Bohemia.

1574—98: Records of the Rosenberg allodial heirs concerning 5060 florins, which were secured by the Bergwert (mine) and the mint in Joachimsthal. The types of claims that were made are shown, for example, by the following:

1571/72: Records concerning the 1200 florins demanded by Georg Christof and Stefan Rüdt von Collenberg, brothers, from the Rosenberg allodial heirs and actually paid, due to their mother Ruffina, the late wife of Knight Albrecht von Rosenberg.

The allodial heirs were four siblings of the von Lagen family, among whom three sisters transferred their claims to the families of von Stetten and others; Eberhard von Stetten received the reverted fief in Sachsenflur from Limburg. The property heirs collectively received the Hohenlohe fiefs, along with the half of Schüpf purchased from the von Dottenheim family, a fief of Kuntes. In contrast, the agnates succeeded to the old Rosenberg half of Schüpf and its appurtenances, in the Palatinate fiefs (parts of Epplingen, Sachsenflur, and Dainbach), and in the Hohenlohe part of Edelsingen along with the church patronage from 1563, as well as in the Würzburg fiefs, namely Hans Eucharius von Rosenberg of Rosenberg, as well as the cousins in Haldenbergstetten.

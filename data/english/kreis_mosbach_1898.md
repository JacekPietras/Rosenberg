Originally probably belonging to the Lords of Boxberg, then to the Knights Hospitaller of Wölchingen, to whom Krafto von Boxberg made the first donation there in 1191\. Later, it appears to have been owned by the Lords of Rosenberg. After the death of Albrecht von Rosenberg (1572), Angelthürn changed hands several times in the following centuries (the longest being owned by the von Dienheim family, until 1668), until 1764, when Joachim Joseph von Fick, a Palatinate-Sulzbach government councillor and court chamberlain, acquired the town for 16,000 florins. With his grandson Franz, who donated his entire estate to the construction of a Catholic church and parish, the family died out in Angelthürn in 1879\. ### **BOXBERG**

Spellings: Bockesberg 1166, Bocchesberg 1172, Bokkeberc 1221, Bocsberg 1301, Poxberg 1489, Poxburg 1528\. Literature: Ottmar F. H. Schönhuth, Bocksberg and the Schüpfergrund near Königshofen, Mergentheim 1856 — C. W. F. L. Stocker, Chronicle of Boxberg... (see above). The lordly family of Boxberg in Baden first appears with Konrad von Boxberg in the second half of the 12th century. Guota von Bocchesberg, mentioned in the Komburg Book of Donations, appears to have been this Konrad's wife. Around 1212, a Konrad von Boxberg was a feudal lord of the Lords of Schweinberg, while Heinrich von Boxberg i. In 1213, the Order transferred the castle and village of Boxberg to the Würzburg Abbey and received it back as a fief. His heirs were the sons of his sister Adelheid: Konrad and Krafto von Krautheim. The latter, renouncing Krautheim, acquired most of the Boxberg estates and henceforth bore the name. With his son Konrad, the family died out in 1313\. The heirs were the Counts of Wertheim, alongside whom the Knights of St. John of Wölchingen, who had founded a commandery here, held the main holdings of Boxberg. The Counts of Wertheim soon ceded their inheritance there to them, but on May 25, 1381, the indebted Order sold the castle and town of Boxberg for 18,000 gold guilders to the knights Konrad and Eberhard von Rosenberg and the noblemen Konrad and Arnold von Rosenberg, who immediately transferred their new acquisition to the Electorate of the Palatinate, in order to receive it back from them as a fief. Rosenberg in Baden is considered the ancestral castle of this family, first documented in 1276\. The descendants of the four brothers, who divided the property among themselves in various ways and heavily fortified the castle and town, increasingly degenerated into robber barons, so that in 1470 the town and castle were besieged by the allied lords of the Electorate of the Palatinate, Mainz, and Würzburg. After Jörg von Rosenberg was expelled, they initially assumed joint control, but in 1477 they restored the former owner under the special condition that the "broken" fortress would not be rebuilt. Jörg von Rosenberg circumvented this prohibition by only rebuilding a bastion; in reality, he created a new castle, the foundation stone of which, according to the inscription (now in Karlsruhe, see below), was laid on the Wednesday before Lent in 1480\. Soon the old hustle and bustle started up again, so that on June 14, 1523 the army of the Swabian League appeared before Boxberg, captured the new castle and reduced it to ashes. Elector Ludwig V of the Palatinate then acquired the lordship of Boxberg from the three sons of Georg von Rosenberg who had fled the castle for 5,000 florins, but already in 1547 Albrecht von Rosenberg, an energetic knight highly esteemed by Emperor Charles V for his outstanding military service, despite the opposition of the Electorate of the Palatinate, regained possession of the property that had been taken from the family, and in the same year the construction of a third castle began, which was inhabitable after just two years. The following years passed with constant disputes between the knight and the Electorate of the Palatinate and the cities of the Swabian League, until in 1548 In 1561, a settlement was reached with the Elector of Heidelberg, resulting in Albrecht von Rosenberg cession of the castle, town, and district of Boxberg to the Electorate of the Palatinate for the sum of 27,000 florins, along with various other compensations. Albrecht later moved his residence to Unterschüpf, where he built a castle (see below). From this time on, Boxberg remained part of the Electorate of the Palatinate.

During the Thirty Years' War, Boxberg fell into Tilly's hands in 1624 (until 1633\) and thereafter alternated between friend and foe, until it was reassigned to the Palatinate in the Peace of Westphalia. From 1691 to 1732, it was pledged to Würzburg and administered by the same, and shortly thereafter, it was also pledged to the Teutonic Order in Mergentheim (until 1740). In 1803, Boxberg passed from Palatinate to Leiningen ownership and then to Baden in 1806\.

The town of Boxberg was formed when the lords of Rosenberg incorporated the village of Wanshoven, located below the castle, into the castle’s fortifications, surrounding it with walls and a moat. First mentioned in documents in 1245, it was already referred to as a town by 1332\. Gate towers mentioned include the Eckmann Gate toward Schweigern and the Cent Gate toward Angeltürn. In 1479, Michel, Jörg, and Arnold Friedrich von Rosenberg granted their town of Boxberg a weekly market to be held on Saturdays. In 1590, the town, which already had three annual fairs, received a fourth one from Elector Friedrich of the Palatinate.

Boxberg was initially likely a subsidiary of Schweigern and later of Wölchingen. Since 1422, it has been listed as an independent parish. As early as 1401, there was a chapel with a benefice dedicated to St. George in the castle. Albrecht von Rosenberg introduced the Lutheran confession, while the Palatinate introduced the Reformed confession, leading to the unification of Boxberg and Wölchingen into one parish. In 1678, Würzburg re-established a separate parish for Catholics. (E.)

The former "fortified house" of the Rosenberg family above the town has now almost entirely disappeared, except for some cellar vaults, wall remnants, and rubble piles (see below). The site, with its magnificent view over the Schüpfergrund, now serves as a municipal festival ground, partially leveled and landscaped (the town owes the donation of the castle grounds to the generous spirit of a benefactor who recently acquired it from private ownership for these public purposes).

As we have seen, there were three castles built in succession on this hill: the first, probably dating from the 12th century, was destroyed in 1470; the second was begun by Georg von Rosenberg in 1480 (see inscription below) but was already reduced to ruins by 1523; and the third, whose foundation stone was laid by Albrecht von Rosenberg in 1547 (see inscription below). Aside from some damage during the sieges and occupations of the Thirty Years' War—the town was surprisingly spared from destruction during the War of the Spanish Succession—this third castle appears to have remained in good condition until the previous century. In 1726, it was even repaired and partly used as a granary under Leiningen's rule (an interesting description of the castle remains from 1821 can be found in Gott: "Schalk: The Knightly Castles and Mountain Castles of Germany," Halle, Vol. V, p. 134). In 1849, the castle was plundered by insurgents, and in 1857 it was auctioned off for demolition and gradually almost entirely dismantled.

The materials for assessing the former size and layout of the castle mainly include 1\. the copper engraving in Matthäus Merian's Topographia Palatinatus Rheni (1645), which we reproduce in Fig. 1, and 2\. a manuscript kept in the registry of the Grand Ducal District Office in Tauberbischofsheim, containing a summary report dated July 25, 1652, intended to provide the Electoral government in Heidelberg with a report on the condition of individual offices after the end of the Thirty Years' War and to make recommendations for the necessary repairs, etc. From this, we extract the following:\*

In Bocksberg, there is 1\. the castle, where the current lord bailiff resides, and which is equipped with a garrison. Upon entering through the first gate, which is equipped with a drawbridge, on the right-hand side is a stone building called the New Building, two stories high, along with a tower that faces directly toward the small town.

Below the building is the stable. On the left-hand side, there is a postern with three vaults on top of each other, and instead of a roof, it is covered with stone slabs along with a small tower where a tower guard was formerly stationed; not far from it, facing the mountain, there is also a walled rondel, which is also covered. When one reaches the second gate, which also has a drawbridge, above which the lord bailiff has his private quarters, on the left-hand side, a building called the Gebäv extends up to the well house, next to which is the uppermost cellar, above which is the armory, and then, next to this cellar, there is another story, in which the sergeant is now lodged, with several steps leading down to the other cellar. On the right-hand side stands the innermost castle, where the offices are located, and one must cross two more bridges, where, outside on the left, the guardhouse stands. Through the antechamber, one enters various corridors that go all around inside, forming the innermost castle courtyard, which is very narrow. The chambers extend on both sides toward the town and out into the fields, except for the offices and a few other rooms, which are completely dilapidated, as well as all the previously mentioned roofing and tiling, especially the tiled roof, which has been damaged three times over the castle, along with beautifully built granaries, making repairs necessary. However, the roof structure itself is still in very good condition almost everywhere, so if the repairs are further delayed, double the damage and costs are to be expected...

From the above report, which leaves nothing to be desired in terms of clarity, it can be deduced that the castle, like all larger medieval fortifications, consisted of two main parts: an outer bailey and a main castle. Two entrances, protected by drawbridges, led into the outer bailey, from which the main castle could only be accessed through another gate, also protected by a guardhouse, drawbridge, and gatehouse. The narrow courtyard of the main castle (the innermost castle) was surrounded by arcades on only two sides, facing the town and the fields, with buildings (the palace and chambers) enclosing them. The keep, which typically would be in the main castle and likely was in the older layout of the castle, appears to have stood in the outer bailey, which, apart from the two-story new building with the stable on the ground floor, included a second utility building and the armory with a cellar on the opposite side. Defense was provided by a three-story vaulted bastion facing the town and a rondel facing the mountain.

The attempt to reconcile the above description with Merian's engraving leads to a somewhat satisfactory result only if we assume that the main access to the castle has always been, as it is now, along the western slope, ascending the front side of the castle hill, where Merian also indicates a palisaded path. The large building to the right of the gate tower would then represent the 'New Building' in the outer bailey, while the tall buildings with the oriel turrets, which are referred to in the report as the 'small tower,' might have given rise to this description due to their towering condition at the time, assuming the draughtsman did not exaggerate. As the residence of the gatekeeper and originally the main defensive work, the tower must have possessed a considerable height.

The recent excavations undertaken by the Boxberg Beautification Association have yielded many interesting results, but they have not yet progressed far enough to justify a survey of the castle complex, which was sold for demolition in 1857 and thoroughly destroyed. Currently, the following remain somewhat preserved: 1\. the aforementioned building facing the mountain, with its massive cellar vaults and the (filled-in) castle well in front of it; 2\. the adjacent "upper cellar" with a well-preserved vault now used as a storage area; and 3\. the "stairs," partly carved into the rock and partly built, leading down between the two buildings to the "other cellar" in the neck ditch.

The remnant of the former keep, referred to by the locals as the Krabben-(Krähen-)Turm (Crab-(Crow-)Tower), is peculiarly located on a rock ledge jutting freely into the neck ditch and falling off steeply on all sides. The material of the castle hill—shell-shaped calcareous slate—made it possible to create such vertical walls to a considerable depth. (Gottschalk describes the condition of the castle in 1821 in Ritterburgen etc. Deutschlands, Vol. V, p. 134; the towers, postern, and ring wall, which were partially still intact at that time, have since been completely demolished or buried down to the last stone.)

Scattered throughout the town are some architectural fragments, window frames, baroque door surrounds, and the like from the castle, such as at the Gasthaus zum Ochsen and the neighboring house (Fratzenkopf). Additionally, in the courtyard of the farmer Jäger, there are allegedly two small reliefs, supposedly from the castle (right side), one of which depicts a fortified medieval town in a woodcut from the 15th century. These are artless works of dubious origin.

The most important remnants of the old castle are currently located in the Grand Ducal United Collections in Karlsruhe. These include: A small plaque (right side) with the Rosenberg coat of arms in high relief, supposedly originating from the castle chapel. An inscription plaque \[according to Leutwein, once above the castle gate; according to Schönhuth, embedded above the entrance to the castle chapel\] with the following content (right side):

"This castle was rebuilt by A. R. in the year of our Lord 1547."

3rd and 4th: Large figure stones (right side) commemorating the rebuilding of the castle by Georg von Rosenberg and his wife (according to Schönhuth, these were also once embedded above the entrance to the castle chapel and were deliberately knocked down in 1848). We see the knight in his house attire, holding what appears to be a measuring rod(?), and on the other stone, his wife carrying baskets and bottles, apparently engaged in providing food and drink to the workers during the construction. The figures are shown in full form and frontal view, somewhat crudely rendered in high relief. Surrounding them is the following inscription, which continues from one stone to the other:

"In the year of our Lord 1480, on Wednesday before Mid-Lent, I, Georg von Rosenberg, Knight, laid the first stone at Boxberg on Monday after St. Michael's Day in the 14th year. Pray to God for us. This stone was laid here by Johann \[von Rosenberg\] of Encholtze, born my lawful wife, and she has faithfully commanded me to complete such construction as it stands here, and it was made as a watchtower into the land."

Of the old city fortifications, towers (see above), and gates, only minor wall remnants on the west side have survived. Even in Merian's time, the walls seem to have been breached by houses in several places, and the towers had been built over.

The old Evangelical parish church, later the property of the Reformed congregation, located on the slope of the castle hill (see the Merian woodcut), was demolished in 1817 after worship services had already been discontinued in the dilapidated building 10 years earlier. A simple bell house now stands in its place. The smaller bell bears the inscription: SANCTA MARIA ORA PRO NOBIS ANNO 1630; the larger bell was cast in 1761\.

At the base of the massive substructure, the gravestone (see right) of Dr. Martin Caspar Brigantinus, Doctor of both Laws, who died in Boxberg in 1637, is affixed.

The Catholic Church (dedicated to St. Aquilinus), located at the top of the town on the main street, was built under Würzburg rule (see above) by Bishop Johann Philipp in 1708, according to Schönhuth, on the site of the former sheep barn over the vaults of a manorial cellar. It is a sober, spacious Baroque building with the year 1764 over the then-renewed southern side portal, and with the bishop's coat of arms over the elevated main portal.

The high altar, with its elegant Rococo decoration, seems to belong to the same period as the aforementioned side portal. The figures of St. Sebastian and St. Aquilinus flanking the tabernacle are excellently carved. The Crucifix in the center with the accompanying angels is less successful.

Two Baroque gravestones inside: one of Franz Joseph Albert Wezel, the Electoral Cologne cellar master who died in 1735, and the other of Ludwig Stockmay, the Palatinate court councilor and cellar master who died in 1767\.

Of the two bells, the older one was gifted by Bishop Philipp in 1726, and the second dates from 1768\.

The town hall is an unadorned building from 1610 (with a round-arched ground floor portal).

The courthouse, formerly the administrative building, on the main street, features a richly decorated Baroque portal with the Palatinate coat of arms, newly erected after the redemption of Boxberg by the Palatinate in 1748\.

A few old residential houses have been preserved; the oldest dates from 1592 near the church, another from 1598, and one from 1600 near the entrance to the castle. Opposite the town hall (at house No. 60\) is a richly decorated stone portal with a gable. The decorations of the double coat of arms and keystone are in a charming Rococo style.

### **UISSIGHEIM**

Spellings: Ussinheim 1165, Ussenheim 1178, Ussenkeim 1220, Uossenkeim 1221, Ussinkem 1288, Ussigheim around 1317, Uessikem 1414\. A separate noble family in the entourage of the bishops of Mainz and Würzburg and the lords of Wertheim, Zimmern, Rineck, and Hohenlohe; first mentioned in a Bronnbach document from 1178: Arnoldus et Conradus, duo filii Ruperti de Ussencheim. According to Bauer (Wirtemb. Franken IX, 179), their property passed through an heir to Eberhard von Rosenberg († 1314), who, in a Seligenthal document from 1288, bears the nickname "von Ussinkeim." The branch of the family that resided in Ussigheim died out with Margaretha von Ussigheim, the wife of Stephan von der Thann. Both sold their farm in Ussigheim and their possessions in the surrounding area to the Bronnbach Monastery in 1457\. Other noble families originating from there are also documented in the 15th and 16th centuries. In addition, ministerials and other nobles had been living there since 1178\. Uissigheim belonged to the Zimmern-Grünsfeld domain. The von Uissigheim family, and as late as 1480 Veit von Riedern, owned tithes here as a Rineck fiefdom. The Mainz fiefdoms, which Ludwig von Rineck gave to Bronnbach Monastery in 1223, were probably originally Zimmern property, as were the properties Bronnbach owned in Uissigheim as early as 1165\. Sovereignty: Mainz (Amt and Cent Külsheim, since 1773 Oberamt Bischofsheim, Vogtei Külsheim). From 1803 to 1806, Leilingen. (E.) Of the old church (a pastor in Uissigheim is mentioned as early as 1223), only the tower, standing south of the current chancel, was retained in the new building of 1846\. Its three lower floors, with their pilaster strips and round-arch friezes, stand out clearly from the two upper floors. Its origin in the late Gothic period is determined by the tracery of the pointed-arch window in the bell tower. In 1602, the tower is documented to have been raised "by one storey." If the tracery does indeed date from this period, we would have here once again (cf., for example, Grünsfeld) a very late example of the tenacious adherence to Gothic forms in church buildings. The date 1666 is inscribed on the clock face. The wooden helmet is modern. The lower tower room, now used as a lumber room, formerly the choir of the old church (here stood an altar of St. Oswald), is square in shape (4.50 m side length) and covered with a Romanesque ribbed cross vault, the apex of which is approximately 7 m above the ground. The shape of the caps with a horizontal apex, the robust pear-shaped profile of the ribs, and the absence of a keystone would indicate the heyday of the Romanesque style, were it not for the admittedly still somewhat crude and awkwardly formed bulbous capitals on the four corner columns, the pressed Attic bases with corner leaves, and the broken form of the shield arches that betrayed the Transitional Style period at the beginning of the 13th century. At the intersection of the ribs, a grotesque figure is carved in relief, and further on one rib, quite immediately, a human head. On the gable wall to the left and right of the entrance are now two old gravestones, one of which bears an old tradition and superstitious veneration Query successful This is the tombstone of the "Black Man"; so popularly called after the black paint with which the gray sandstone slab is painted (see Plate XVIII). The stone formerly rested on two feet in the middle of the church. In 1641, the grave was opened; it was not built of stone, but merely dug into the earth. Turkish shackles (compedes Turcicas) and a few bones were found inside, "proof that the body had been exhumed earlier." Father Josephus Hartmann (later Abbot of Bronnbach), pastor of Uissigheim from 1691 to 1696, had the stone lifted and moved to the Epistle side because it was hindering processions. In the process, the pieces that are now missing were lost; they had already been knocked off by a falling beam during repairs to the church in 1639\. During the new construction in 1846, the stone was placed in its current location after the corners had been added. (E.) In addition to those mentioned, various other damages remain. Most disturbing, apart from the missing front, i.e., upper part of the little man holding the sword, is the destruction of the figure's feet and the lower edge with the name, mainly caused by the superstition of the population, who scraped dust from them to feed to livestock in cases of illness. The inscription reads:YEAR · LORD · M · 300 30 /////// \[IV\] YOU COME · MILES · OF · VSSINKE · 18 KL /////.  This is therefore the gravestone of a knight from the noble family of Uissigheim, who died as a young man in the 1430s or 1440s. According to some (cf. Kaufmann, see below), his name was Fritz; however, the Jesuit Gamans (d. 1679), who visited Uissigheim shortly after 1641 and had the pieces that had been broken off two years earlier (see above) but were still there at the time brought back and reassembled, renders the inscription (see the papers of Pastor Severus in the Mainz City Library) as follows: Anno dni M CCC XXXVI subiit gladio beatus Arnoldus iuvenis miles de Vssicke XVIII kl. decbr. According to this, it would be the gravestone of a knight named Arnold von Uissigheim, as popular tradition often calls him "the blessed Arnold." Both names appear repeatedly in the 14th century. For example, the necrology of Bronnbach Abbey (see Abtila, I, p. 7\) records a Frizo de Uissigheim as armiger on October 5, 1324; another Friedrich appears in documents from 1328, 1332, 1336, and 1343, also always with the title armiger. An Arnold appears from 1305 to 1332, a second from 1323 to 1363\. The interpretation of the peculiar depiction is just as uncertain as the identification of the individual. The small figure emerging from the stone, holding a sword across the knight's neck, is only present in the lower part. The upper body, which was once attached by a dowel, is missing, and this rules out the possibility of verifying whether a Jew was depicted and whether the old tradition is correct, according to which the knight was treacherously attacked and killed in the forest by the Jews whose extermination he had set himself in Uissigheim. The Erfurt chronicler (Mencken, Rerum Germ. III p. 338\) relates: In 1343, Jews were killed in the towns of Rödingen (Röttingen), Aub, and Bischofsheim, as well as in many other towns and villages. The instigator of these persecutions was a knight from Vssingheim. He once heard a Jew blaspheme against the Holy Sacrament as it was being carried past, and swore to do everything in his power to help murder the Jews, which he did. The Jews persuaded Lord Gottfried von Hohenloch, with a gift of 400 pounds of Heller, to capture von Uissigheim. This happened; he was captured and taken to Röttingen, where he received the Holy Sacraments very often and devoutly, and was then beheaded in Kitzingen. The body was taken to his hometown of Uissigheim and buried in the church there. Countless miracles occurred at his grave. (Wibel, Hohenlohe Church History, Onolzbach 1752 I, 249). (E.) Nothing is known for sure about the origin of the Romanesque parish church, located on the north side of the lovely Schüpfergrund above the village. Bauer considers it unlikely that the Knights of St. John built the church, as they only owned a commandery in Wölchingen in 1249, and the architectural style indicates an earlier date. In his opinion, however, "the respected lords of Boxberg" could well have been the founders of this building. This is contradicted by the fact that the Boxbergs never erected similarly sumptuous buildings either in their residence above or in Wanshofen, located at the foot of the castle. Furthermore, the Knights of St. John had owned large estates near Boxberg and Schweigern even earlier, namely since 1192, although a farm in Wölchingen is only documented in 1239\. Nevertheless, it is much more likely to attribute the construction of this church, which can be seen from afar as a symbol of their power and which virtually dominates the Schüpfergrund, to the rich members of the order, who were well versed in architecture and art, than to the minor dynasts of Boxberg. Frater Conradus sacerdos, mentioned in 1249 in Wölchingen, was certainly also appointed by the Order, so the parish must have been founded by the Order a generation or two earlier. In the liber Synodalis Wirceburgensis, the parish (Chapter Mergentheim), Frühmüsse B. M. V. and the vicarage of St. Johannis in Wölchingen are mentioned for the year 1452\. Now the Protestant parish church for Boxberg and Wölchingen. The construction records of the Grand General State Archives on our church begin in 1663, but essentially contain only information of little relevance to the building's history, concerning repairs to the venerable building, which had apparently been badly damaged during the preceding war years. Regarding the condition of the building at the time when the Protestant building authority began its most recent repairs, the construction officer, Baurat Behaghel, in Heidelberg, wrote the following: "The existing structure at that time was not suitable for systematic graphic recording, as it presented a far from pleasing image, and moreover, the original form of the building was almost obscured from view by a conglomerate of the most primitive structural makeshift structures. These makeshift structures, which, however, survived for centuries, consisted of superstructures above the only fragmentary main cornice, which, judging by their overall nature, had probably served defensive purposes since its destruction during the Peasants' War, and were composed in detail of fragments of the former tower and gable superstructures, or even of clay timber framing. The old roof truss was poorly constructed from thin, not even square-jointed logs and had been patched up many times over the past few centuries. The bells, which hardly dated back to the previous century, had been hung on the south side of the granary in an ordinary oak trestle frame as a bell cage. The interior of the church looked downright desolate; it was completely built over with colorful, marbled, randomly placed wooden galleries, to which numerous steps led. It had been so diminished with paint and crude, rustic paintings from the end of the previous century that the forms of the capitals were barely recognizable, and the entire church gave the impression of a large junk room. The walls of the north side, which were embedded at a height of about 3 meters in the muddy soil of the old cemetery at the back, were covered with dense green lichen along the entire length of the church, almost up to the level of the vaults, and were so wet that even to this day, the traces of this have not completely disappeared. The crypt was also covered with mud up to the height of the windowsills. Before the restoration, the vaults, made of tuff, were partly in very poor condition, as their plaster was almost completely destroyed due to the hygroscopic properties of the material. As far as the restoration of the church was concerned, the first step was to thoroughly clear and dry the area, along with extensive drainage. Then, all superstructures above the main cornice had to be removed, the previous gables had to be restored, and a crossing tower had to be constructed. After that, the entire interior, after removing all obstructive fixtures, had to be exposed and restored to its original form. In addition to the unsuccessful searches for possible remains of the foundations of a particular bell tower, the examination of the crossing pillars, whose foundations exhibit a quite unusual depth and thickness, as well as individual remains of wall supports at the relevant locations in the attic, all of which supported the earlier existence of a crossing tower. Further evidence came from the various debris found in the superstructure, which, after eliminating the pieces undoubtedly suitable for the cornices, gable ends, and the like, made their former use in a tower structure seem very likely. The restoration of the central and transept nave sills was facilitated by the unexpected discovery of well-preserved fragments of the starter and cornice stones of the arched frieze on the west gable, as well as by the excavation of several different motifs from the arched inlays embedded in the vault rubble. The reconstruction of the entire roof structure was presented so clearly that the present form of the church's roof structure can be considered completely identical to its earlier form, especially since the finds also provided numerous clues regarding the details of the crossing tower. Furthermore, a close resemblance to the Romanesque buildings of Lower Franconia has been observed. The cleaning and restoration of the interior, especially the pillars, capitals and vault ribs, and indeed all the stonework, was an extremely laborious and time-consuming task, as several coats of paint (including oil paint) were superimposed and had virtually grown into the porous stone material, so that a complete overhaul by the stonemason and striking of the surfaces with a stock hammer proved necessary. During the entire restoration, nothing was added except for the gallery in the west of the nave, which was essential for the organ installation. The principle was strictly adhered to: to expose or supplement what was already there and to reconstruct what was missing. Even with regard to the painting, which has suffered greatly as a result of the dampness left over from earlier times and is to be restored in a more durable manner in the near future, the work was limited to the simplest surface decoration in order to better emphasize the architectural elements in their dignified simplicity. The restoration work began in 1877 and was completed in 1878\. (The term "cloister" in a contract dated February 8, 1731, refers, as is clear from the context, to the central section of the nave and transept, forming a cross.) Building description. The church is a Romanesque, three-aisled, cross-shaped pillar basilica with a semicircular apse and two side apses, vaulted in a tied system (see floor plan Fig. 67). The dimensions are not large, but the proportions are so fortunate that the building appears larger than it is. The whole thing must have been erected quickly according to a uniform plan; only in one place can a deliberate deviation from the original layout be detected. After the first bay of the nave – construction began in the east as usual – was completed, it seems that people came to the conclusion that the strongly projecting pillars of the side aisles unnecessarily narrowed them and that the side walls were sufficiently strong to support the small vaults. At the same time, the distance between the cumbersome arcade pillars was reduced from 1.66 to 1.86 m. 1.35 m to 2.00 m, thus giving the western part of the nave a freer, more airy character (see Fig. 68). The main apse, like the side apses, is vaulted with a semi-dome. In all three, at the front corners, at moderate intervals below the impost, which is accentuated by a cornice, there is a flat cuboid with a straight, simply decorated front. It projects slightly like a console, allowing a slight narrowing of the semicircle in the upper parts. This arrangement has never been encountered by the author before, and the purpose of which is unclear, since, given the existing dimensions, such a small saving in span would not have been possible, even for the large semi-dome, let alone for the side apses. The seven square cross vaults that cover the choir, transept, and nave are of identical construction, although they have the same width (an average of 5 meters). Broad, unarticulated, heavy-duty transverse arches separate the vaults, whose ribs appear simple with a sturdy pear-shaped rib, and only in the chancel are they somewhat more richly profiled, with a bulge and pear-shaped rib. The ribs of the southern transept vault show a remarkable deviation: the edges of the square stones, projecting far in front of the coping, are chamfered and set with hemispheres on the slope, an original but somewhat heavy-duty motif. \[The above-mentioned bell cage stood above this until 1877\]. The keystones show simple arched sections between the ribs and flat-pattern decoration, and are circularly hollowed in the center. While the transverse arches of the nave are supported by the capitals of the half-columns, the diagonal ribs emanate from consoles that are joined to the column capitals and covered by a common abacus. Some of them have a piece of column shaft, decorated with grotesques or similar motifs on the underside (see Fig. 69). In the choir and transept, however, the diagonals of round columns also rise in the corners. There are no special shield arches anywhere; instead, the capping line, i.e., the shield arch line (incorrectly depicted as a semicircle in our sections), which forms a steep basket arch at a significant rise in the apex, lies directly against the surface of the shield wall. Otherwise, the pointed arch form prevails everywhere, both in the transverse and diagonal arches, as well as in the arcade arches. The cover plate of the half-column capital and the consoles extends as a transom cornice around the entire "cloister" (see above). A second, strong horizontal emphasis is formed by the transom cornice of the arcade arches, which curves around the half-column supports. The clumsy arcade pillars stand on a richly structured base consisting of cornice, round bar, plate, slope and foot, while the base cornice running all the way around the walls has only a round bar and foot plate. In contrast to the somewhat cumbersome ribbed cross vaults of the "cloister," the vaults of the side aisles—two of which, in keeping with the tied system, are arranged for each central bay—are spanned without ribs on corner consoles between heavy, unprofiled transverse arches. The latter, too, are supported only in the first two bays (see above), and in the rest by simply decorated corbels. In the southeast corner (at the transept) and northwest corner (at the west front), corner columns are exceptionally installed, consistent in both aisles. The passage from the side aisle to the transept has undergone a peculiar narrowing on both sides, as a second pillar with an arch has been inserted within the opening corresponding to the transverse arch. At the end of the north aisle, a spiral staircase cut out of the strong front wall leads to the church floor. The windows, like the doors (see below), are all round-arched and smoothly cut into the walls, without rods or the like at the corners and with only a moderate slope. In the transept, the aforementioned impost cornice runs to a dead end on both sides of the upper windows; in the nave, this disadvantage was easily avoided by setting the windows accordingly higher (see Fig. 68). The round windows in the gables are particularly attractive. The one above the apse appears to be framed by a strong "Norman" zigzag and has a panel pierced all around by circular cutouts. Ornamental decoration is used with extreme sparingness in the interior; except for the capitals and consoles, and to a limited extent on the keystones, there are no ornaments to be found anywhere. The forms generally adhere to the Romanesque canon, but in some places, attempts at bulbous forms and a freer, more natural treatment of the foliage and tendrils are already apparent. The transitional period is thus revealed not only in the vaulting (pointed arches), but also in the intrusion of Gothic forms. The interior is of great beauty and will be significantly enhanced once the planned painting (see above) is completed and the uniform, cold tone of the stone has disappeared. Despite all the compactness and coarseness, which is particularly evident in the arcades and vaulting, and despite all the poor ornamentation, the eye still wanders with pleasure through the high nave and the bright transept to the choir, where the colorfully glazed round window above the concha illuminates effectively. In terms of clarity, clarity, and uniformity of its interior, the St. John's Church in Wölching undoubtedly ranks first among the oldest churches in Franconia and Swabia (see collotype plate XIX). Of the details inside, it is worth mentioning: above the sacrament niche in the choir there is a completely mutilated relief of the Crucifixion, which, judging by the shape of the letters, dates back to the 14th century. The adjacent door leads outside. The sacristy may once have been located in this corner, as usual, and is mentioned repeatedly in the building records of the General State Archives, but without further details. A now bricked-up door located at the top (about 4 meters above the floor) in the west wall of the northern transept probably provided access from outside to a gallery of the Knights of the Order and may have been directly connected to the hospital above. Beneath half the choir and the apse is a low crypt, accessible via a narrow staircase through an opening in the floor. Its shape is circular, covered by a circular vault supported by a central pillar (see Fig. 71). Four slanted slit windows provide the small space with minimal illumination. Of particular interest is the octagonal capital of the central column (see Fig. 72), from which eight ribs extend and gradually taper into the round barrel vault. That the unusual circular shape of the crypt in this case, as so often, is connected with the general idea of ​​the circular structure of the Holy Sepulchre (cf. Dehio and Bezold, die kirchliche Baukunst des Abendlandes, Vol. I, Stuttgart 1892, pp. 35 ff., 542 and 554 ff.), seems just as irrefutable as the assumption that the Knights Hospitaller Commander would have had their tomb here. The exterior of the church reflects the interior: uniform, simple, yet beautiful and stately. The restoration work, carried out with great piety, is noticeable only in the currently somewhat lighter color of the material used. The walls, constructed of rubble stone, are unstructured and decorated only with simple but robust main and base cornices. The latter runs horizontally around the entire building, regardless of the variation in the floor level. In contrast to the interior, the exterior is still dominated exclusively by round arches. The windows also appear without frames or moldings, with the exception of the apse windows, which have robust round-bar frames. Apart from the southern side portal, only the three apses, the west facade, and the gables are somewhat more richly decorated. Actually, however, it is only a round-arch frieze here, which has a stimulating effect and displays the same decoration by means of volute-like insets or underlays as in the apse of the Bronnbach monastery church (see Section I, Plate I). Here, the stonemason had a great opportunity to explore his wealth of forms and ideas, to demonstrate his imagination in diverse ornamentation and figurative grotesque creatures. (The motifs of the above-mentioned finds have been very skillfully used in the restored sections.) The gable ends also appear to be decorated in part with grotesques and squatting figures, the cover plates with a round-arch pattern and diagonal cut (see Fig. 73). In keeping with the overall simplicity, the main portal and south portal (both completely intact) are richly structured, yet treated with extreme restraint in ornamentation and a complete renunciation of figurative decoration. The south portal on the front side of the church (see collotype XX) can be described as a classic example of noble and simple design. Particularly attractive is a bas-relief of triangular tendrils above the apex of the arch, below an equal-armed cross crowning the gable. To achieve a colorful effect, the round columns in the jamb of the main portal were made of red sandstone, and the polished limestone cylinder on the south portal was painted red in a similar manner, as the existing paint remains demonstrate. Traces of paint are also visible elsewhere, on the tympanum stone, capitals, and bases. The front facade was provided with a (wooden?) vestibule in the usual manner. The traces of the pilasters are still clearly visible in places. The asymmetrical arrangement of the pilasters bordering the nave on the main facade is very odd. The left-hand projection is about 0.30 m closer to the main portal than the right-hand one, so that a corresponding projection to the left had to be made above the projection line of the northern aisle roof in order to reach the corner of the rising nave at the top. That this was the correction of an oversight when marking out the left half is obvious. Otherwise, the right-hand pillar projection would not have been moved away from the ashlar edge of the portal by the specified width from below, thus avoiding the unsightly ledge that resulted. The presence of the aforementioned spiral staircase on the left side inside the wall has nothing to do with this. On the rear, it can be described as faulty, or at least as odd, that the tip of the semi-conical roof of the main apse projects freely in front of the wall into the curve of the window above. Furthermore, the technical aspects of the exterior have also been treated with the utmost care. The modern, eight-sided crossing tower contributes significantly to the overall impression and effectively enlivens the silhouette.

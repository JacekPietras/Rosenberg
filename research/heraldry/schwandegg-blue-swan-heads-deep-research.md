# Schwandegg arms with two outward-facing swan heads

## Finding

The blue paired-swan design is real and can be inspected in **Niklaus Gatschet, *Wappenbuch des alten und neuen Adels der Schweiz*, volume 1, page 11** (1799), in the Zürich section. The page shows two silver swan necks and heads facing outward on a blue shield. A modern Swiss armorial catalog assigns the blazon “In Blau zwei abgewendete, silberne Schwanenköpfe” to **von Schwandegg, Zürich** and cites Gatschet (1799), but gives no page-level citation. Page 11 is the matching primary image. The caption is most plausibly **Schwandegg**, while **Schwanegg** cannot be ruled out. The catalog may rely on this same caption rather than independent evidence, so its attribution does not settle the reading. [Gatschet, p. 11 scan](https://viewer.burgerbib.ch/Bilddateien/Mss_h_h_XIV_58_master/Mss_h_h_XIV_58_p11.JPG); [DFG-Viewer catalog record](https://dfg-viewer.de/show?cHash=25cf6ffa1f2a36f12d51c56c9fab3a4a&tx_dlf%5Bdouble%5D=0&tx_dlf%5Bid%5D=https%3A%2F%2Fburgerbib.k-r.ch%2FshowXml%2F189&tx_dlf%5Bpage%5D=1); [Wappensammlung.ch, Schwandegg entries](https://www.wappensammlung.ch/wappensammlung/wappen-s-t/schupbach-seigneux?start=180)

This proves that the design was represented in a Swiss armorial by 1799. On renewed inspection of the full-resolution page, the handwritten label appears to read **Schwandegg**, although **Schwanegg** cannot be excluded. The modern catalog's attribution to the Zürich von Schwandegg is therefore plausible and is probably based on this very caption. It still does **not** prove that the swans were the medieval arms of the lords of Schloss Schwandegg. The direct medieval evidence consistently identifies the historical Schwandegg arms as an ibex:

- In the **Zürcher Wappenrolle** (c. 1335–1345), the arms attributed to von Schwandegg are a standing ibex, with an ibex head as crest. The relevant isolated original is cataloged as ZUR-065; visual inspection confirms an ibex, not swans. [ZUR-065, Zürich armorial image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:ZUR-065.jpg); [Clemmensen, transcription of the Zürich roll](https://www.armorial.dk/german/Zurich%20WR.pdf)
- A **1347 seal of Niklaus von Schwandegg**, described on p. 182 of *Thurgauisches Urkundenbuch*, vol. 5, document 1886, has “Im Dreieckschild ein schreitender Steinbock” (a passant ibex in the triangular shield). The seal is attached to the original parchment held at the Zürich State Archives (StA.Z., Stadt und Landschaft C I, no. 208). This is contemporary material evidence for the family’s arms. [*Thurgauisches Urkundenbuch*, vol. 5, p. 182 (PDF)](https://hvtg.ch/skin/frontend/PDF/publikationen/Urkundenbuch_Bd_5.pdf)

The best-supported conclusion is therefore: **Gatschet's 1799 page probably labels the blue, outward-facing swan pair “Schwandegg”; “Schwanegg” is a live alternative.** The attribution to a Zürich Schwandegg name is likely, but its connection to the medieval lords of Schloss Schwandegg is unproven and conflicts with the c. 1340 roll and 1347 seal. No seal or monument with the paired swans was located.

## Reading the Gatschet caption on page 11

I re-examined the full-resolution master image (1536 × 2304 pixels), rather than the earlier small crop. In the bottom row of the page headed **Zürich**, the blue shield with two outward-facing swans has its own handwritten caption immediately below it. My best reading is **“Schwandegg”**; **“Schwanegg”** remains a plausible alternative because the medial `d` is not fully distinct. I do not read the sequence as “Dehmundagh” in the full-page context. More than visual resemblance supports an initial **S**: the Burgerbibliothek's METS structural map categorizes p. 11 under section **S**, between p. 10 (P, R) and p. 12 (T). That independently rules strongly against a P- or D-initial reading, though it does not distinguish Schwandegg from Schwanegg. For handwriting comparison, I checked the target against the same volume's capital **P** in “Pestalozzi” on p. 10 and a **D**-initial label on p. 3; those forms have different opening strokes from the target's broad, looped **S**. I also compared the target's lower-case sequence with the other handwritten labels in p. 11's S section. The middle group is compatible with `chwan`/`chwan-d`, but the cramped minims make the user's `mund` segmentation understandable; the terminal strokes are compatible with `egg`, although the final descenders are too compressed to make that ending certain in isolation. The alphabetic section evidence is considerably stronger than these letter-shape comparisons.

The identification is strengthened by the page context: the manuscript's marginal heading reads **Zürich**, the nearby entries are Zürich arms, and *Schwandegg* is a historically documented Zürich name. Heinrich Meyer explicitly lists **Schwandegg, Gemeinde Stammheim** separately from **Schwanegg bei Horgen** (entries 960–961). He says only the Schwanegg castle was named for its knight's arms, who bore a swan in his shield. That distinction keeps both readings live: `Schwandegg` fits the place/family and the modern armorial attribution, while `Schwanegg` would fit Meyer's swan-bearing knight. The Zürich page contains a sequence of individual painted shields and handwritten labels, not a prose list that spells out each attribution; the digital structural map supplies only page-level alphabetic sections. Thus the manuscript context strongly establishes an S-name but does not independently resolve the `d` or intended lineage.

The modern Swiss armorial catalog describes a Zürich von Schwandegg variant as “In Blau zwei abgewendete, silberne Schwanenköpfe” (in blue, two silver swan heads turned away from one another) and cites Gatschet's 1799 volume. It also lists one-head swan and ibex variants under Zürich von Schwandegg. This supports Schwandegg as the leading reading, but does not independently identify Gatschet's handwritten label: the catalog cites the volume without a page, and its attribution may ultimately derive from this very page. The two swans in Gatschet's image are drawn with necks and upper bodies rather than as severed heads, so the catalog's blazon is a heraldic abstraction, not a literal transcription of the drawing.

No exact matches for the proposed spellings **Dehmundagh**, **Sehmundagh**, or **Pehmundagh** surfaced in the catalog and indexed historical sources searched. This negative search is weak evidence by itself; the likely explanation is that the cursive was segmented differently, not that those forms can be ruled out absolutely.

**Revised caption confidence:** moderate for `Schwandegg`; low-to-moderate for the distinction between `Schwandegg` and `Schwanegg`; low for `Dehmundagh`/`Sehmundagh`/`Pehmundagh` as literal readings. A clear photograph of the original leaf or a parallel copy of the same arms and label would be needed to settle the name.

An additional source suggests a possible route for later confusion, but does not prove one. Heinrich Meyer’s *Die Ortsnamen des Kantons Zürich* (Zürich, 1849), entries 960–961, treats **Schwandegg, Gemeinde Stammheim** and **Schwanegg bei Horgen** as separate places. Meyer derives Schwandegg from a cleared forest slope, while he says the Schwanegg castle was named after its knight’s arms, “er führt nemlich den Schwan im Schild.” This is evidence for a swan-bearing knight associated with the similarly named Schwanegg, not for the von Schwandegg of Stammheim. It makes conflation a plausible hypothesis for a later secondary attribution, but there is no demonstrated link from Meyer (or this Schwanegg knight) to the Gatschet shield. [Meyer, *Die Ortsnamen des Kantons Zürich* (1849), e-rara scan](https://doi.org/10.3931/e-rara-66193)

## Source trail and dating

The Burgerbibliothek Bern identifies the manuscript as Mss.h.h.XIV.58, *Wappenbuch des alten und neuen Adels der Schweiz* (1), dated 1799. Its scan labels the matching leaf “Pag. 11” and heads it “Zürich.” The page image verifies the blue field and outward-facing pair; close inspection supports reading the lower handwritten caption as “Schwandegg,” though the medial letters are not perfectly distinct. The Burgerbibliothek's METS structure groups p. 11 under **S**, p. 10 under **P, R**, and p. 12 under **T**. These are page-level headings rather than an index of the individual shields, but they provide independent confirmation of the initial letter. The modern Wappensammlung entry cites Gatschet's 1799 volume but supplies no folio, so it does not document its attribution work.

The transmission history also cautions against treating the 1799 catalog as direct evidence for medieval family arms. A 1952 discussion of Gatschet’s volume notes from its preface that Gatschet was not its compiler: he credited the non-Bernese arms to the Zürich seal engraver Bullinger. The author suggests Bullinger’s multi-volume armorial at Zentralbibliothek Zürich as the immediate model and says Bullinger gathered many arms from his seal collection. This is a plausible source chain for Gatschet, but the suggestion has not been traced to a specific Bullinger volume/page or to an earlier Schwandegg seal showing swans. [1952 discussion of Gatschet and Bullinger](https://selfranga-prod11.ethz.ch/cntmng?pid=szg-006%3A1952%3A2%3A%3A911)

Meyer’s 1849 distinction is particularly useful because it excludes treating the names as interchangeable: the historical Schwandegg at Stammheim is one entry; Schwanegg near Horgen, with a swan-bearing knight in Meyer’s account, is another. The 1799 Gatschet page still cannot be securely assigned to either without a readable caption or a traceable exemplar. The Horgen connection should therefore be reported as a possible explanation to test, not as an established identification.

By contrast, the Zürich roll is materially verified: image ZUR-065 shows an ibex in the shield and an ibex as crest. Clemmensen transcribes this as “swandeg” and records an ibex with border. A general modern Schloss Schwandegg article labels the image as the lords’ arms; its caption is consistent with this visual check. The 1347 seal provides an independent contemporary confirmation of an ibex shield. [Direct ZUR-065 scan](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:ZUR-065.jpg); [Thurgauisches Urkundenbuch, vol. 5](https://hvtg.ch/skin/frontend/PDF/publikationen/Urkundenbuch_Bd_5.pdf)

## Family and place distinction

The likely intended historical family is the **von Schwandegg of the Zürich area**, associated with Burg/Schloss Schwandegg near Waltalingen in the present canton of Zürich. The castle history page says the family is attested by 1288 and became extinct in the male line in 1413. [Schloss Schwandegg history](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schloss_Schwandegg)

However, “Schwandegg” is not a unique heraldic label. The Wappensammlung itself lists distinct Schwandegg families/entries associated with Zürich, Schaffhausen, and Luzern. Some carry a black or silver ibex; the Zürich entries include a single swan head, two swan heads, and an ibex. This is a warning against treating every arm under the surname or place name as one continuous medieval lineage. The blue/two-head arms may be an authentic armorial attribution but its exact bearer/line remains uncertain.

## Image verification and monuments

 - **Blue field and orientation:** visually confirmed on Gatschet, p. 11. Two silver swan necks face outward on blue.
 - **Caption/bearer:** the page's handwritten label most likely reads “Schwandegg”; “Schwanegg” remains possible. The modern catalog attributes the paired-head blazon to von Schwandegg, Zürich, but this may derive from the same page rather than independent evidence.
 - **Medieval arms:** high confidence that the historical lords of Schwandegg used an ibex by 1347; this is shown in both the c. 1340 roll and a contemporary seal description.
 - **Monuments:** no paired-swan monument located; no claim of such evidence is supported.
- **Name-confusion possibility:** Meyer (1849), entries 960–961, explicitly separates Schwandegg/Stammheim from Schwanegg/Horgen and assigns a swan shield only to the latter’s knight. This is suggestive context, not proof of the source of the Gatschet attribution.

## Confidence and open checks

**High confidence** that the blue outward-facing swan pair appears on Gatschet p. 11 (1799). **Moderate confidence** that the handwritten caption reads “Schwandegg”; **low-to-moderate confidence** in distinguishing it from “Schwanegg.” The catalog's Zürich von Schwandegg attribution is plausible but not independent if it relies on Gatschet. **High confidence** that the medieval Schwandegg arms attested by the Zürich roll and Niklaus's 1347 seal use an ibex. The unresolved historical question is whether the later swan arms belong to the Zürich von Schwandegg, a Schwanegg-related bearer, or reflect a cataloging conflation. A matching Bullinger exemplar could resolve that source question.

## Reassessment of the attribution and source chain

The direct catalog record identifies the manuscript as *Wappenbuch des alten und neuen Adels der Schweiz*, vol. 1, by Niklaus Gatschet, 1799, shelfmark Mss.h.h.XIV.58, held by the Burgerbibliothek Bern. The DFG-Viewer contents divide the volume into regional sections; Zürich begins at page 1. The independent catalog entry for the paired-head blazon is grouped under Zürich von Schwandegg and cites the Gatschet 1799 source record. Because that source record is for the whole volume and the scan at p. 11 is the matching image, the catalog is corroborative of the Schwandegg reading but is not demonstrably independent of the manuscript caption.

The caption's leading letter is best read as S: the page belongs to the Zürich sequence and the manuscript's letter-section/catalog structure places the relevant entry within S. `Schwandegg` fits both the visual reading and the modern catalog's attribution; `Schwanegg` remains possible because the compressed middle letters and the distinction between `d` and no `d` are not perfectly clear. The user's proposed `Dehmundagh`/`Sehmundagh`/`Pehmundagh` readings do not fit the catalog's S-section evidence as well, but letterform alone is not conclusive. Heinrich Meyer's 1849 place-name study explicitly distinguishes Schwandegg near Stammheim from Schwanegg near Horgen and reports a swan-bearing knight for the latter; that makes Schwanegg a real alternative worth preserving, not a resolved correction.

The medieval control evidence is against identifying this image straightforwardly with the lords of Schloss Schwandegg: the Zürich Wappenrolle and Niklaus von Schwandegg's 1347 seal show an ibex, and a modern historical discussion likewise notes the Schwandegg lords' ibex arms. Later or parallel arms could differ, but no pre-1799 seal or monument with the paired swans has yet been found. The 1952 discussion of Gatschet's compiler/source chain points toward the Zürich seal engraver Bullinger's armorial work, but no matching Bullinger page or earlier seal has been identified. Thus the best current conclusion is: **the page probably labels the swan arms Schwandegg, and the Zürich von Schwandegg attribution is plausible; the exact reading, bearer, and lineage relationship remain unproven.**

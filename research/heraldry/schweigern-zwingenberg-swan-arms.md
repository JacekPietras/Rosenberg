# Schweigern and Zwingenberg: swan heraldry and possible connections

Research scope: Schweigern in the Boxberg district and Zwingenberg on the Neckar (Neckar-Odenwald-Kreis), with name-confusion checks for Zwingenberg an der Bergstraße and Schwaigern in the Kraichgau. The focus is swan imagery as shield charge versus crest, its attestation, and any demonstrable relationship to Rosenberg/Uissigheim, Hunebourg, or Schwandegg/Schwanegg.

## Findings at a glance

1. **Schweigern (Boxberg) has no swan in its documented municipal arms.** Its arms reproduce the two-part court-seal design attested in 1516: the Rosenberg red-and-silver bars on the dexter half, and a gold half with red bar and gold rose on the sinister half. The official Boxberg account says the second arms is not identified. The armorial component derives from Rosenberg authority/association, not from swan imagery.
2. **Zwingenberg on the Neckar does have swan-neck shield charges:** three silver swan necks, arranged 2:1, on blue, with gold beaks. The municipality adopted the arms in 1913, taking the design from nineteenth-century municipal seals; LEO-BW records this blazon. Local historical accounts attribute the design to the medieval lords of Zwingenberg. It is a three-charge shield, not a pair of outward-facing necks.
3. The earliest securely retrievable visual/textual witness to the **three-neck family shield** located in this search remains nineteenth-century civic seal use, reported retrospectively by LEO-BW; the 1913 municipal arms formalized that design. A 1926 account explicitly attributes three silver swan necks in a blue shield and one swan neck as crest to the medieval lords, but gives no medieval image or citation for the blazon. A separate 1958 account refers to an early seal as bearing a **Reiherhals** (heron neck), without reproducing it. The latter may concern Wilhelm von Wimpfen, rather than the later-named Wilhelm von Zwingenberg: the early seal evidence cannot yet be securely assigned or identified to species from an image.
4. **A separate Zwingenberg exists at the Bergstraße in Hesse.** Its historic civic arms are a Katzenelnbogen lion over three sea-leaves, not swans. Its similarly named medieval lords are explicitly distinguished from the Neckar Zwingenberg family in the European Burgeninstitut's EBIDAT entry. Do not merge the two places or dynasties.
5. I found no genealogical evidence tying the lords of Neckar Zwingenberg to Rosenberg/Uissigheim, Hunebourg, or Schwandegg/Schwanegg. Shared use of bird-neck charges is not evidence of kinship. The plausible near connection is the Kochendorf–Zwingenberg lineage: the Neckar castle's first known lord, Wilhelm von Zwingenberg, is identified in modern castle histories as a nephew of the Wimpfen bailiff Wilhelm; LEO-BW describes the Kochendorf ministerials as probably descended from Wilhelm von Wimpfen. This is a regional/kinship connection within the Neckar-Odenwald/Heilbronn setting, distinct from heraldic motif similarity to the other families.

## 1. Schweigern in the Boxberg district

### Second-pass audit of the 1338 lead and regional overlap

The best-matched 1338 Rosenberg record is the Würzburg Landgericht entry of 23 July, not a Schweigern charter. The entry survives in the original paper register, Staatsarchiv Würzburg, Standbuch 822, fol. 126v, and summons *Eberhardus de Rosenberg* and other men in a debt/pledge case. The text neither names Schweigern nor describes, announces, or bears an individual seal; it cannot be a visual witness for the swan arms ([WB01, no. 575](https://www.medieval-ashkenaz.org/WB01/WB-c1-00ac)). Eberhard's identification with the regional Rosenberg/Uissigheim house is plausible from his separately documented Dürn office and family activity, but that does not connect the 1338 proceeding to Schweigern.

A useful regional association surfaced at Wildenberg: the University of Würzburg's *Historisches Unterfranken* database records brothers Wiprecht, Wilhelm, Wicker, and Dietrich von Zwingenberg as castle officials there in 1332, and Eberhard von Rosenberg as an official there in 1343. This puts the Zwingenberg and Rosenberg men in the same regional administrative network a decade apart; it is evidence of institutional proximity, not of kinship or a shared heraldic origin ([Wildenberg entry and dated events](https://www.historisches-unterfranken.uni-wuerzburg.de/burgen/burgen-einzeln.php?burg=146)).

The other relevant 1338 reference is Zwingenberg am Neckar's opening-right dispute. It explains the date in local histories, but so far yields no surviving, viewable 1338 charter seal with a swan. The more precise documentary sequence is the November 1339 truce and the January 1340 inquiry; the latter is known through an edition/regest, not a directly inspected charter image. The civic arms' three swan necks are documented in later municipal seals and adopted in 1913; the visual is available from [LEO-BW](https://www.leo-bw.de/en/detail/-/Detail/details/DOKUMENT/labw_wappen/6201/Wappen%2Bvon%2BZwingenberg). They should not be backdated to the 1338 dispute absent a contemporary image.

The possible early Zwingenberg seal identification also remains species- and person-uncertain. Liebig's 1958 article uses *Reiherhals* (“heron neck”) and its sentence is ambiguous about whether the seal belongs to Wilhelm of Wimpfen or Wilhelm of Zwingenberg ([article, p. 153](https://regionalia.blb-karlsruhe.de/frontdoor/deliver/index/docId/26164/file/BLB_Badische_Heimat_1958_2.pdf)). Sigilla's image for the 1240 Wilhelm von Wimpfen seal depicts a single bird neck, not a pair ([seal record](https://sigilla.irht.cnrs.fr/11189), [image](https://sigilla.irht.cnrs.fr/iiris_img/private/image/FRAD067_0151NUM0107_03.png)). It is therefore excluded as proof of two swans.

### Arms and earliest evidence

The city of Boxberg’s official town-district page blazons Schweigern's arms: a divided shield, with the front half itself divided and five times per pale argent and gules counterchanged, and on the rear gold half a red fess bearing a gold rose. It says the present arms are based on the court seal of 1516, whose left side was Rosenberg and whose right-side arms remains unknown ([Stadt Boxberg, “Schweigern”](https://www.boxberg.de/stadt-boxberg/die-stadtteile)).

The local arms survey transcribes the seal's use on 2 October 1516 by the Schultheiß, court, and community and gives its legend as *S. S. VND GERICHT SCHWEIERN*. It says the die was likely older, and that the municipality adopted the seal devices and archival-recommended colors on 1 June 1923 ([Ortswappen.de, Schweigern](https://www.ortswappen.de/index.php?buchstabe=J&land=Baden-Wuerttemberg&site=10&site_i=15&wahl=6709)). The current shield's Rosenberg half is independently recognized in Boxberg's town history, which explains that Schweigern and Kupprichhausen preserve Rosenberg's red-and-silver checks/bars ([Stadt Boxberg, “Literarischer Heimatweg”](https://www.boxberg.de/freizeit-kultur/wandern-radfahren/literarischer-heimatweg)).

No swan is present in this blazon or in the cited 1516 seal description. The gold-and-red rose/bar compartment remains unidentified; it should not be assigned to a family or treated as a swan charge without new evidence.

### 1338 check: Rosenberg name, but no heraldic image

A dated 1338 record does name a local Rosenberg: on 23 July, Eberhard von Rosenberg and several other men were summoned before the Würzburg land court over the complaint of Lewe, son-in-law of Abraham of Weimar, and Lewe's wife. The edition identifies its source as the original German/Latin paper register, Staatsarchiv Würzburg, Standbuch 822, fol. 126v, and prints the entry as *Würzburger Landgericht* II-3086 ([Medieval Ashkenaz, WB01 no. 575](https://www.medieval-ashkenaz.org/WB01/WB-c1-00ac)). The later procedural note says some defendants supplied pledges after 8 September. This is a court-book record, not a separately sealed charter; the edition provides no seal description or heraldic image. It names Eberhard von Rosenberg but does not mention Schweigern, identify a charge, or document the paired swan heads. Thus it is a genuine 1338 Rosenberg record in the regional context, but cannot be cited as visual evidence for swan heraldry.

The nearest directly photographed archival object found in this check is earlier: Heinrich von Fleckenstein's 1336 pledge deed lists property at Schweigern among several places. The SigiAl/Archives d'Alsace record identifies the original as Ar Grand-Est, 1 J 41 and names four sealers: Heinrich de Fleckenstein, Anshelm de Grünenberg, Walraf de Seltz, and Friedrich de Seebach ([catalog record with document photograph](https://sigilla.irht.cnrs.fr/sigial.php/151640)). Its date is 1336, not 1338, and none of the named sealers is identified there as a paired-swan bearer. The deed supplies an actual image lead for Schweigern's 1330s property history, but not the sought swan object.

#### Who is Eberhard, and is this the “1338” clue?

The 1338 entry gives only *Eberhardus de Rosenberg* and lists Rosenberg (Baden-Württemberg) in the place index; it does not provide a title, office, estate, or residence at Schweigern/Boxberg. Identification with the Rosenberg family of the Boxberg region is plausible, but not proved by this entry alone. A local scholarly account of Arnold von Uissigheim identifies Eberhard II (active 1328–1347) as a Mainz bailiff at Dürn and son of Eberhard I, *miles de Rosenberg dictus de Ussinkeim*; it also names Eberhard von Rosenberg, Vogt zu Dürn, among the sealers of a 1332 agreement involving the Uissigheim/Rosenberg circle ([Arnold von Uissigheim study, PDF](https://uissigheim.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/ARNOLD-VON-UISSIGHEIM.pdf)). This makes the family identification credible, while still leaving the 1338 register entry itself non-specific.

Nearby records further associate this Eberhard with Mainz and Dürn, not specifically with Schweigern: a 9 July 1334 expense account survives as Hauptstaatsarchiv München, Mainz, Erzstift fasc. 77 no. 154a; the LAGIS regest says it is uncertain whether Eberhard's seal was attached ([LAGIS regest 10675](https://www.lagis-hessen.de/index.php/de/subjects/xsrec/current/1/sn/lgr?q=YToxOntzOjU6IkfDpG5zZSI7fQ%3D%3D)). A 1323 Aschaffenburg charter explicitly lists Ritter Eberhard von Rosenberg among the sealers, but its online metadata has no image ([bavarikon, StiftsA U 1697](https://www.bavarikon.de/object/bav%3AAAB-URK-00000BAV80035799?cq=&lang=de&p=1)). I found no viewable impression or independent heraldic image securely attributable to him from these records. Consequently, the 23 July 1338 item could explain a remembered *date plus Rosenberg name*, but it does not supply the alleged swan image, and its subject is a court summons in a Jewish debt/pledge case rather than a Schweigern monument or arms record.

### Place-name trap: Schweigern versus Schwaigern

The similar place-name **Schwaigern** is a different town west of Heilbronn. Its association is with the Neipperg family: the *Deutsche Biographie* describes the Neipperg lineage as probably going back to Birtilo of Schwaigern, and records the family's acquisition of the Kraichgau lordship of Schwaigern in 1302 ([Deutsche Biographie, “Neipperg, von”](https://www.deutsche-biographie.de/sfz70981.html?language=de)). LEO-BW describes Schwaigern's arms as three silver rings on red and a St John the Baptist shield-holder ([LEO-BW, “Wappen von Schwaigern”](https://www.leo-bw.de/fr/detail/-/Detail/details/DOKUMENT/labw_wappen/2178/Wappen%20von%20Schwaigern)). This is neither the Schweigern near Boxberg nor a swan-bearing design. Search results for “von Schweigern” can easily drift into the Schwaigern/Neipperg material.

## 2. Zwingenberg on the Neckar: the swan-neck arms

### Chronology of the swan-neck arms and the 1338 date

The documentary chronology does **not** currently establish 1338 as a date for the arms. The often repeated 1338–1340 dates concern the construction of the Mainz fortress of Fürstenstein during the dispute with the Zwingenberg lords; they are castle-history dates, not dates on a swan-bearing seal, shield, or monument. The armorial trail instead has three distinct levels:

| Date | Evidence | What it establishes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1253 | A Wilhelm von Zwingenberg is reported in a Wimpfen hospital gift context, but modern research cautions that this mention cannot securely be tied to the Neckar castle; Kochendorf's castle Zwingenberg is a closer geographic possibility. No matching swan-bearing seal impression has been located. | Neither a secure first mention of the Neckar lords nor an attestation of their swan arms. See the discussion in *heilbronnica 5*, n. 60. |
| 19th century | The LEO-BW Ortslexikon says the three-neck arms reappeared in the community seal in the 19th century, after an 18th-century Agnus Dei seal. | Earliest currently reported civic use of the three-neck composition. This is later than the medieval lordship and does not by itself prove the medieval attribution. |
| 1834 | Carl Cäsar von Leonhard, *Fremdenbuch für Heidelberg und die Umgegend*, II, p. 800, says the arms of the Hirschhorn lords could be seen on one of the castle towers. | A pre-1843 notice of a castle armorial display, but it explicitly belongs to the post-1403 Hirschhorn ownership context; it does not describe or depict the earlier Zwingenberg family's swans. The digitized page is text-only at this location. |
| 1843 | A Google Books page image previously identified as a title-page vignette shows a shield with three bird-neck shapes and one in the crest. | A visual witness, but the species and any direct medieval source behind the image need comparison with the original page and its caption. The prior working identification as swans is not equivalent to a contemporary blazon. |
| 1913 | The General State Archives Karlsruhe redesigned the seal image as a formal coat of arms; the municipality adopted it shortly afterward. | Official civic adoption of the familiar arms. LEO-BW blazons three silver swan necks 2:1 on blue with gold beaks. |
| 1926 | Ph. Pfl., “Schloß Zwingenberg im Neckartal,” *Badische Heimat* 13 (1926), p. 95. | Explicitly says the medieval lords bore three silver swan necks in a blue field and one swan neck as crest. This is a clear early-20th-century attribution, but the passage cites no medieval armorial, seal, or monument. |
| 1958 | A *Badische Heimat* account calls a seal of Wilhelm a heron neck (Reiherhals). | A conflicting species identification for an early seal; no image accompanies the reference in the passage located. It should not be harmonized with the 1926 swan blazon until the exact seal and its owner are identified. |

The 1926 article is especially useful because it separates shield and crest: **three** swan necks belong to the shield; **one** neck is the crest. Its source is [MARCHIVUM digital copy, p. 95](https://druckschriften-digital.marchivum.de/download/pdf/113831.pdf). The same page gives the historical narrative: the Zwingenberg lords held the castle as a Hohenlohe fief; the first written mention of the castle is given as 1326; a 1343 arbitration settled the toll dispute and ordered Fürstenstein demolished; Zwingenberg was destroyed in 1364. The 1338–1340 Fürstenstein construction interval falls within this conflict narrative but carries no armorial claim.

LEO-BW supplies the civic arms history and tinctures ([Zwingenberg Ortslexikon](https://www.leo-bw.de/en/detail-gis/-/Detail/details/ORT/labw_ortslexikon/6201/Zwingenberg)); it says the three-neck device returned in nineteenth-century seals and was formalized by the Generallandesarchiv in 1913. The direct digital record for the civic blazon is [LEO-BW, Wappen von Zwingenberg](https://www.leo-bw.de/en/detail/-/Detail/details/DOKUMENT/labw_wappen/6201/Wappen%2Bvon%2BZwingenberg). These are good evidence for the modern civic coat and its official colors, but not primary evidence for the medieval family's arms.

Seyler's *Abgestorbener Württemberger Adel* (1911), p. 195, is a further armorial/genealogical lead, accessible through the [GDZ scan](https://gdz.sub.uni-goettingen.de/id/PPN83027040X). The scan has a separate plate section; this search has not established a plate image or cited medieval source that would independently move the swan blazon before the nineteenth-century civic seals. A later Wikipedia family article cites Seyler and states that both Kochendorf and Zwingenberg branches used silver swan necks, but it should not be treated as an independent primary witness ([article and bibliography](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kochendorf_(Adelsgeschlecht))).

### Search for pre-1843 images, seals, and monuments

I checked the digitized 1605 *New Wappenbuch* index entries available for B and S and did not find an entry under Zwingenberg; the CERL/Siebmacher general index is the appropriate fuller index for the later and expanded editions ([CERL index description and access](https://dbis.uni-regensburg.de/UBU/resources/101841)). This is a bounded negative result, not proof that no relevant arms could occur under a different family name. The 1911 Seyler volume is later than the 1843 vignette and provides no earlier image by itself.

Leonhard's 1834 local guide is the only pre-1843 heraldic notice located in this pass. It records a coat of arms on a castle tower but calls it the arms of the Hirschhorn lords, whose tenure began after 1403; it does not describe swan necks. The page's text does not reproduce a seal or shield image ([digitized edition, II, p. 800](https://doi.org/10.11588/diglit.14875#0114)). Thus I have not found a pre-1843 visual witness to the three swan-neck shield. The currently earliest image located remains the 1843 title-page vignette, and its identification rests on the armorial subject/caption, not on an earlier medieval original.

The castle's surviving chapel and wall paintings are also not evidence for the pre-1843 family arms without a matching swan image and secure attribution. The castle was rebuilt by the Hirschhorn after 1403 and its chapel was consecrated in 1424; the accessible inventories and descriptions located concern that later building phase and do not report the three swan-neck shield ([EBIDAT castle record](https://www.ebidat.de/cgi-bin/ebidat.pl?id=5767); [Deutsche Stiftung Denkmalschutz summary](https://www.denkmalschutz.de/denkmal/schloss-zwingenberg.html)). I found no medieval tomb, effigy, or carved shield attributable to the earlier Zwingenberg lords in the catalog searches made for this branch.

I also searched the open web and Sigilla for the four personal names supplied as the 1368 brothers (Hans, Wilhelm, Beringer, Wiprecht), including combinations with *Siegel*, *sceau*, and *Zwingenberg*. I found no Sigilla record or image that could be securely assigned to any of the four. The searches did surface a separate record for four brothers **Wiprecht, Wilhelm, Wicker, and Dietrich Zwingenberg as Amtmänner at Burg Wildenberg in 1332** ([Historisches Unterfranken record](https://www.historisches-unterfranken.uni-wuerzburg.de/burgen/burgen-ergebnisse.php?eintrag=700)); it is not a 1368 seal record and names a different set of brothers. Neither the 1332 entry nor its secondary citation supplies arms. Until a dated charter or archive reference for the 1368 quartet is identified, no seal image should be attributed to them on the basis of names alone.

### Blazon and heraldic position

The official state heritage portal LEO-BW gives the municipal blazon: **“In Blau drei (2:1) silberne (weiße) Schwanenhälse mit goldenen (gelben) Schnäbeln”**—on blue, three silver swan necks, arranged two over one, with gold beaks ([LEO-BW, “Wappen von Zwingenberg”](https://www.leo-bw.de/en/detail/-/Detail/details/DOKUMENT/labw_wappen/6201/Wappen%2Bvon%2BZwingenberg)). This is a charge group on the shield. It is not a helmet crest and it is not the two-head composition of Cod.icon. 309. The municipal depiction can be inspected at [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wappen_Zwingenberg_(Baden).svg).

LEO-BW is the strongest currently located source for the precise official tinctures and blazon. *FOTW*, citing the state land-information system and information from the local administration, reports that this was the family arms of the lords of Zwingenberg, revived in nineteenth-century seals and adopted by the municipality in 1913; it describes the same azure field and three argent swan heads with gold beaks ([FOTW, Zwingenberg (Baden)](https://www.fotw.info/FLagS/de-mh-zw.html)).

An older local history of the Neckar castle, indexed by the Mannheim city archive's digitized print collection, describes the builders as the nobles of Zwingenberg and says they bore **three silver swan necks on a blue field and one swan neck as crest**. It contrasts them with the unrelated lords of Zwingenberg at the Bergstraße, whose arms were three red hearts on gold with a half lion above ([MARCHIVUM, digitized periodical/article](https://druckschriften-digital.marchivum.de/download/pdf/264040.pdf)). This is a useful explicit distinction and a possible crest account, though the publication is an early twentieth-century secondary source, not a contemporary medieval record.

### Medieval lords and earliest textual/image evidence

The Neckar castle was built around 1200; the lords of Zwingenberg are documented from 1253. The Deutsche Stiftung Denkmalschutz summarizes that the lords lost the castle in the mid-fourteenth century and the fortification was slighted in 1364; the fief passed to the lords of Hirschhorn in 1403 and the castle was rebuilt from 1410 ([Deutsche Stiftung Denkmalschutz, Schloss Zwingenberg](https://www.denkmalschutz.de/denkmal/schloss-zwingenberg.html)). The European Burgeninstitut's EBIDAT gives 1253 as the first mention of Wilhelm von Zwingenberg and identifies him as nephew of the similarly named Wimpfen bailiff; it also explicitly says Neckar Zwingenberg has no relation to the Bergstraße namesake ([EBIDAT, “Zwingenberg am Neckar”](https://www.ebidat.de/cgi-bin/ebidat.pl?id=5767)).

A 1958 *Badische Heimat* article by Fritz Liebig states that Wilhelm's frequently used seal showed the “Zwingenberg arms with the heron neck” (*Reiherhals*), then notes that Wilhelm of Zwingenberg is described in 1253 as *nepos Wilhelmi advocati Wimpfensis* ([BLB Karlsruhe, *Badische Heimat* 1958/2, p. 153](https://regionalia.blb-karlsruhe.de/frontdoor/deliver/index/docId/26164/file/BLB_Badische_Heimat_1958_2.pdf)). This is early heraldic evidence as reported by a twentieth-century historian, but two points remain unresolved:

- **Species:** the article says *Reiherhals* (heron neck), while later local descriptions and official arms say swan necks. Stylized bird necks on small seals can be difficult to distinguish; the author's wording should not be silently normalized.
- **Attribution:** the passage has Wilhelm von Wimpfen as the subject of the sentence immediately before “his seal,” but then gives the 1253 kinship statement about Wilhelm von Zwingenberg. The article may be referring to a Zwingenberg-family seal, or may have conflated the two Wilhelms. Direct comparison with a catalogued seal impression is needed before claiming that the 1253 seal proves the full three-swan-neck blazon.

The primary record for the January 1253 grant by Wilhelm the Vogt of Wimpfen says two seals were formerly attached, but it supplies no image or description of their devices ([LEO-BW, WUB V, no. 1248](https://www.leo-bw.de/de/web/guest/detail-gis/-/Detail/details/DOKUMENT/labw_wub/1870/Wilhelm%2Bder%2BVogt%2Bvon%2BWimpfen%2B%C3%BCbergibt%2Bdie%2BH%C3%A4lfte%2Bseiner%2BWiese%2Bbei%2BUntereisesheim%2Ban%2Bdas%2BHeiliggeistspital%2Bin%2BWimpfen%2Bzum)). A different original deed of December 1250 by the same bailiff has an editorial description of his personal gem seal: two riders and three wolves, not bird necks ([LEO-BW, WUB IV, no. 1161](https://www.leo-bw.de/media/labw_wub/current/delivered/pdf/wub-1764.pdf)). The key correction is that the 1958 article's grammar is clearer than initially thought: “his frequently used seal” refers to Wilhelm von Wimpfen, whose career it is discussing; the following sentence separately notes that a nephew called Wilhelm von Zwingenberg in 1253. Thus its “Zwingenberg arms with a heron neck” is not evidence for the nephew's seal, still less for the three-swan shield. Also, *heilbronnica 5*, n. 60 explicitly cautions that the 1253 Wilhelm mention in connection with the Wimpfen hospital gift cannot confidently be assigned to Neckar Zwingenberg; the nearby Kochendorfer Zwingenberg is another plausible referent ([Stadtarchiv Heilbronn, *heilbronnica 5*](https://stadtarchiv.heilbronn.de/fileadmin/daten/stadtarchiv/online-publikationen/16-heilbronnica5.pdf)). The 1253 evidence therefore establishes neither the Neckar family arms nor even the castle identification.

The local municipal coat of arms appears in seals from the nineteenth century and was formally adopted in 1913, according to the arms-history account. I did not locate a medieval armorial image or a directly viewable medieval seal image that unambiguously confirms all three necks, their swan species, and colors. A 1958 history offers a singular bird neck in a seal (using “heron”); the precise bridge from that seal to the later three-charge shield therefore remains a research gap.

### The 1338 reference: opening rights, not a swan image

The likely 1338 lead concerns **Zwingenberg on the Neckar**, not Schweigern and not Zwingenberg an der Bergstraße. LEO-BW's historical-place entry states that the castle, held as a Ganerbschaft by the Zwingenbergs, was first opened to the Archbishop of Mainz in 1315 and in 1338 had to be opened jointly to Mainz and the Count Palatine. It further says Archbishop Heinrich von Virneburg built Fürstenstein above the castle, but it was dismantled in 1340 after a Palatine protest ([LEO-BW, Zwingenberg history](https://www.leo-bw.de/detail-gis/-/Detail/details/ORT/labw_ortslexikon/6203/Zwingenberg)). The independent castle-history entry EBIDAT likewise connects the forced opening of the Neckar castle with Heinrich's subsequent counter-castle Fürstenstein ([EBIDAT, Fürstenstein](https://www.ebidat.de/cgi-bin/ebidat.pl?id=5768); [EBIDAT, Zwingenberg](https://www.ebidat.de/cgi-bin/ebidat.pl?id=5767)). A modern historical study of Louis the Bavarian's regional dispute summarizes later documentary testimony: in the inquiry recorded in January 1340, Zwingenberg's castlemen testified that they were ministerials of the Mainz archbishopric. This is evidence about jurisdiction and feudal allegiance, not a description of the family arms ([Königliche Gerichtsbarkeit und regionale Konfliktbeilegung…](https://dokumen.pub/knigliche-gerichtsbarkeit-und-regionale-konfliktbeilegung-im-deutschen-sptmittelalter-die-regierungszeit-ludwigs-des-bayern-13141347-abt-b-abhandlungen-zur-deutschen-rechtsgeschichte-1nbsped-9783428545445-9783428145447.html)).

I have not located a 1338 charter facsimile, a surviving seal image attached to the opening-rights instrument, or a heraldic depiction specifically dated 1338. The 1338 event therefore **does not currently furnish visual evidence for swans**. The nearest directly viewable relevant image is the later municipal shield (three silver swan necks on blue), shown and described by [LEO-BW's arms record](https://www.leo-bw.de/en/detail/-/Detail/details/DOKUMENT/labw_wappen/6201/Wappen%2Bvon%2BZwingenberg); LEO-BW explains that this design was revived in nineteenth-century local seals and formalized in 1913. For the 1338 counter-castle, an image of the surviving Fürstenstein ruins is available in the [Wikimedia Commons image category](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Burg_F%C3%BCrstenstein_(Zwingenberg)), but it is a modern photograph of masonry and contains no heraldic carving. Do not caption either as an image of a 1338 swan-bearing seal.

### Targeted follow-up: what is actually dated 1338?

The best-supported identification is **the opening-rights dispute involving the Neckar castle**, not a tomb, effigy, or surviving heraldic monument. LEO-BW's state gazetteer says the lords had to open Zwingenberg to Mainz in 1315 and, in 1338, to Mainz and the Palatine jointly ([LEO-BW, Zwingenberg Ortslexikon](https://www.leo-bw.de/detail-gis/-/Detail/details/ORT/labw_ortslexikon/6203/Zwingenberg)). The Geo-Naturpark's local history independently associates 1338 with the end of the Zwingenberg lords' ability to control river traffic and collect tolls ([Geo-Naturpark Bergstraße-Odenwald, Zwingenberg am Neckar](https://geo-naturpark.net/kommune/zwingenberg-am-neckar/)). A modern scholarly study gives the documentary chronology with greater precision: the dispute intensified in the late 1330s; a royal truce was issued on 30 November 1339; and Count Rudolf of Wertheim reported the result of an inquiry on 8 January 1340, in which Zwingenberg's castlemen testified to their status as Mainz ministerials. The study cites the primary record as *Regesten der Erzbischöfe von Mainz* (REM), no. 4471, and Würdtwein, *Nova subsidia diplomatica*, vol. 5, no. 87 ([study, pp. 198–199 and nn. 963–970](https://dokumen.pub/knigliche-gerichtsbarkeit-und-regionale-konfliktbeilegung-im-deutschen-sptmittelalter-die-regierungszeit-ludwigs-des-bayern-13141347-abt-b-abhandlungen-zur-deutschen-rechtsgeschichte-1nbsped-9783428545445-9783428145447.html)). This places the useful seal-bearing/charter trail in the **1339–40 dispute dossier**, but the source as summarized concerns testimony and feudal allegiance, not arms.

This distinction matters because some popular castle summaries compress the sequence into a single “1338 opening-rights” episode and say the Zwingenbergs signed. The scholarly chronology instead documents a 1339 truce, a January 1340 inquiry, and subsequent competing arbitration instruments through June 1340. I could not verify a 1338 original or locate an image of its seal. Würdtwein's cited edition is discoverable as a digitized work, but the currently indexed e-rara PDF is only a short extract from another part of the multivolume work, not vol. 5 no. 87 ([e-rara catalog/digital extract](https://doi.org/10.3931/e-rara-22548)); it cannot serve as a visual check of the relevant item. The 1843 printed source, Krieg von Hochfelden, *Die Veste Zwingenberg am Neckar*, is catalogued by LEO-BW ([bibliographic record](https://www.leo-bw.de/en-GB/detail/-/Detail/details/DOKUMENT/bsz_swb/1100794840/Die%20Veste%20Zwingenberg%20am%20Neckar%20ihre%20Geschichte%20und%20ihr%20gegenw%C3%A4rtiger%20Zustand%20G%20H%20Krieg%20von%20Hochfelden)); its pp. 125–129 publish later 1340 arbitration acts, not a newly identified tomb or 1338 armorial object.

The 1338–40 castle conflict is therefore a plausible *context* for swan-bearing Zwingenberg lords, and later sources attribute the three-neck arms to that family, but it does not itself establish what their arms looked like. The next direct test is to obtain the archival originals or full editions of REM 4471 and the related 1339–40 instruments, identify the named Zwingenberg castlemen and their seals, then compare the impressions against the 1958 *Reiherhals* report. No newly found 1338 monument or swan image can responsibly be added on present evidence.

The online evidence found for Schweigern does not identify a parallel 1338 swan event. Schweigern's securely described court seal is from 1516, and its documented arms have no bird charge (see section 1). Thus “1338” appears to be a Zwingenberg castle-history lead; if the user's lead refers to a different 1338 object (such as a particular charter, seal, or monument), its repository or quoted description will be needed to identify it without conflating it with the opening-rights episode.

### Visual audit: swans only; exclude lookalikes and unrelated objects

The visual criterion here is a clearly identifiable swan head/neck or swan, not a vaguely bird-shaped charge and not an animal inferred from a place-name.

**Confirmed swan image associated with the lords (1843):** Georg Heinrich Krieg von Hochfelden's *Die Veste Zwingenberg am Neckar* (Frankfurt, 1843) has a title-page vignette showing a shield with three clearly stylized bird necks and a single-neck crest. The actual page image is viewable in [Google Books, title page](https://books.google.com/books?id=JEw4AAAAYAAJ&pg=PP9&source=gbs_selected_pages&cad=1); [direct page image](https://books.google.com/books/content?id=JEw4AAAAYAAJ&hl=de&pg=PP9&img=1&zoom=3&sig=ACfU3U2nZsjdorN-q8Yscm2I-eHmp0uW8A). The book's 1843 imprint and title identify this as the Zwingenberg castle's heraldry, although that vignette alone does not name the birds. A 1926 illustrated-periodical account explicitly identifies the medieval castle's builders as the Edlen von Zwingenberg and describes their shield as three silver swan necks in a blue field, with one swan neck as crest ([MARCHIVUM digitized article, p. 95](https://druckschriften-digital.marchivum.de/download/pdf/113831.pdf), lines 1739–1744 in the text transcription). Together, the family-specific 1843 drawing and the explicit 1926 identification establish that the depicted forms are meant as swan necks, not unicorns. LEO-BW independently records the later civic blazon and says the family arms reappeared in nineteenth-century municipal seals before the 1913 formal adoption ([LEO-BW arms record](https://www.leo-bw.de/en/detail/-/Detail/details/DOKUMENT/labw_wappen/6201/Wappen%2Bvon%2BZwingenberg)). This is real visual evidence for the Zwingenberg swan tradition, but it shows three necks and is not an image of a two-swan shield or a 1338 object.

**Confirmed swans in the 1330s Schweigern context:** None. The photographed 1336 pledge deed concerns holdings at Schweigern, but its surviving seals are not identified as swan-bearing; no readable swan device has been established from its photograph. The separate 23 July 1338 Würzburg court-book entry naming Eberhard von Rosenberg is not a sealed charter and has no heraldic image. The 1516 Schweigern court-seal blazon is a divided shield with Rosenberg bars and a rose-and-fess compartment, not swans.

**Exclude the 1380s Hünenberg grave slab from the swan evidence:** The monument image previously cited as a follow-up is a Hünenberg/Hunenberg object, not Schweigern or Zwingenberg. The shield is described and pictured with unicorn heads; it does not show swans and must not be presented as a swan monument or as evidence for the 1338 clue. Likewise, a 1240 Wimpfen seal described as a heron neck is not a swan witness. The genuine Hunebourg 1248 seal is a separate, visually identifiable swan-crest example (paired necks above a per-fess shield), but it is unrelated to the Schweigern/Zwingenberg 1338 records.

**Finding:** The 1843 title-page vignette is a direct image of Zwingenberg arms with three neck charges; read with the explicit 1926 blazon, these are swans and belong to the heraldic tradition attributed to the Edlen von Zwingenberg. I have not found an image of a swan-bearing 1338 charter, seal, grave, effigy, or monument for Schweigern or Zwingenberg. The Hünenberg unicorn slab, Wilhelm von Wimpfen's separately catalogued 1240 heron seal, and the dark/indeterminate 1336 deed seals do not meet the criterion.

### Community arms derived from Zwingenberg

The swan charges were used as territorial memory by several former municipalities in the lordship's Odenwald lands. Waldbrunn's official site says that Strümpfelbrunn's former arms recalled the three Zwingenberg swan necks, and that the current arms of Strümpfelbrunn, Mülben, Weisbach, and Waldkatzenbach carry three silver necks on blue in their upper field, explicitly interpreting them as signs of former membership in the lords of Zwingenberg's lordship ([Waldbrunn, Strümpfelbrunn](https://www.waldbrunn-odenwald.de/waldbrunn/gemeindeportrait/ortsteile/struempfelbrunn); [Mülben](https://www.waldbrunn-odenwald.de/waldbrunn/gemeindeportrait/ortsteile/muelben); [Weisbach](https://www.waldbrunn-odenwald.de/waldbrunn/gemeindeportrait/ortsteile/weisbach); [Waldkatzenbach](https://www.waldbrunn-odenwald.de/waldbrunn/gemeindeportrait/ortsteile/waldkatzenbach)). Weisbach's history additionally says it belonged to the Zwingenberg lords when first documented in 1326 and that its local seal shows the three necks from the Zwingenberg arms ([Weisbach history](https://www.weisbach.info/entstehung)). These later municipal derivatives attest the regional afterlife of the device; they do not add medieval evidence about the family's original shield.

## 3. Other places called Zwingenberg

### Zwingenberg an der Bergstraße (Hesse)

This is a separate city and separate medieval lordship. Its historical arms are not swans: the local historical society describes the oldest known city arms, attested in 1350, as a divided shield with the Katzenelnbogen lion above and three hearts below; later forms use three sea-leaves below the lion ([Geschichtsverein Zwingenberg, “Stadtgeschichte”](https://geschichtsverein-zwingenberg.de/zwingenberg-stadtgeschichte/)). The Mannheim archival periodical explicitly contrasts the Bergstraße lords' hearts-and-lion arms with the Neckar lords' swan-neck arms ([MARCHIVUM article](https://druckschriften-digital.marchivum.de/download/pdf/264040.pdf)). EBIDAT says there is no connection between the two place names/families ([EBIDAT](https://www.ebidat.de/cgi-bin/ebidat.pl?id=5767)).

### Kochendorfer Zwingenberg (Bad Friedrichshall)

There is also the castle/estate called Zwingenberg at Kochendorf, today Bad Friedrichshall. This is not the same castle as Zwingenberg on the Neckar. LEO-BW states that ministerials of Kochendorf are attested from 1253 and probably descended from the Wimpfen bailiff Wilhelm; they held the Kochendorfer castle called Zwingenberg on the Lindenberg ([LEO-BW, “Kochendorf”](https://www.leo-bw.de/fr/detail-gis/-/Detail/details/ORT/labw_ortslexikon/1800/Kochendorf%2B-%2BAltgemeinde~Teilort)). The broader family summary cites Seyler's *Siebmacher* and distinguishes a Kochendorf line from a Zwingenberg line; its account says they bore three silver swan necks ([Seyler, *Abgestorbener Württemberger Adel*, p. 195, digital edition](https://gdz.sub.uni-goettingen.de/id/PPN83027040X); summary with bibliography: [Kochendorf family](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kochendorf_(Adelsgeschlecht))). This provides a plausible kinship framework between Kochendorf and Neckar Zwingenberg, but it is not a connection to Rosenberg, Hunebourg, or Schwandegg.

The identity and heraldic development merit further direct source work: examine Seyler p. 195 and its plate, the manuscript seal/sphragistic evidence cited by the 1958 article, and early seals of the Kochendorf/Zwingenberg branches. Current web evidence does not let us date the three-neck family shield to the 1253 mention with certainty.

## 5. Monuments and image evidence

I found **no newly verified medieval tomb, effigy, or monument of the Neckar Zwingenberg lords bearing the three swan necks or a two-neck charge**. The extant castle's current heraldic displays are mostly associated with later owners (notably Hirschhorn and later seigneurs); modern town arms and community arms are evidence for the later civic adoption, not medieval monuments. The 1958 *Badische Heimat* claim about a seal is a promising lead but needs image-level confirmation. For exact monument work, next targets are the 1424 castle chapel's surviving heraldic stone/paint, the 1410–15 rebuilt portions of the castle, and tombs/epitaphs of the Zwingenberg line or its heirs. No object should be counted as a swan monument until the carving is visually inspected and its provenance identified.

## Follow-up: earliest located images and the Siebmacher check (2026)

The earliest **viewable image** located in this branch remains the title-page vignette in Georg Heinrich Krieg von Hochfelden, *Die Veste Zwingenberg am Neckar* (Frankfurt, 1843). The drawing has three necked bird charges in the shield (two above, one below) and a single neck in the crest. The line drawing alone is not species-diagnostic, but the subject is corroborated by a later explicit blazon: the 1926 article in *Badische Heimat* 13 identifies the Edlen von Zwingenberg as bearing three silver swan necks on blue and one swan neck as crest. Links: [Google Books title page and image](https://books.google.com/books?id=JEw4AAAAYAAJ&pg=PP9&source=gbs_selected_pages&cad=1) · [direct vignette image](https://books.google.com/books/content?id=JEw4AAAAYAAJ&hl=de&pg=PP9&img=1&zoom=3&sig=ACfU3U2nZsjdorN-q8Yscm2I-eHmp0uW8A) · [1926 article PDF](https://druckschriften-digital.marchivum.de/download/pdf/113831.pdf), printed p. 95.

The much-cited 1368 notice is earlier evidence for the **actual medieval seals**, but is not an image: in the 1843 appendix, Krieg says the four brothers' seals were attached and that each shield had a *Schwanenhals*. His text provides no drawings, count per shield, or current archival shelfmark. The two 1338 charters likewise have seals attached according to the edition, but no heraldic device is described. Therefore, no accessible actual medieval seal impression has yet been verified visually in this research.

The earlier suggested Siebmacher lead does not yield an earlier image. I checked Gustav A. Seyler, *Abgestorbener Württemberger Adel* (1911), p. 195, and its associated plate 105 in the [GDZ scan](https://gdz.sub.uni-goettingen.de/id/PPN83027040X). Page 195's “Kochendorf” entry is genealogical prose and does not describe swan arms; plate 105 does not depict a Zwingenberg/Kochendorf swan shield. It is therefore not an independent heraldic witness and should not be cited as visual evidence for this family. The later assertion that both branches had three silver swan necks may be right, but the cited Seyler page/plate does not substantiate it.

**Current conclusion:** the 1843 vignette is the earliest located image of the family arms; Krieg's account of attached 1368 seals is the earliest located report of extant medieval seal impressions, but those impressions remain unpictured and unlocated. The 1926 source explicitly identifies the 1843-style charge as swan necks and supplies the three-in-shield/one-in-crest arrangement. No verified paired-swan design is involved: the shield has three neck charges, not two heads facing outward. This branch has not found a pre-1843 armorial image or a medieval monument carrying the charge.

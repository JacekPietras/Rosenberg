# Internet search: armorial descriptions of two outward-facing swan heads/necks

Search date: 20 October 2026

## Scope and criterion

This search looked beyond the project image catalogue for published armorial descriptions in English and German. The target is two swan heads/necks as charges in the shield, turned away from each other (German *abgewendet*, *voneinander abgewendet*; English *addorsed*). It excludes inward-facing/*respectant* pairs and pairs that occur only as a crest. A text description is recorded separately from a surviving or viewable image.

## Strongest additional historical-family lead: the Lords of Huneburg (Alsace)

The digitized *Jahrbuch der K.-K. Heraldischen Gesellschaft Adler* has a detailed entry for the Alsatian Lords of Huneburg. It describes their arms as a horizontally divided black-and-gold shield, with two silver swan necks with red beaks, turned away from one another, in the upper part; the crest is also two silver swan necks. The same passage says the older seals show only the divided shield and the two necks as crest, and that the necks were only later incorporated into the upper shield. This is a clear textual match to the requested shield charge, with an important chronological qualification: the pair was not on the earliest seals.

The digitized 2020 archaeological/heritage monograph *Burgruine Hünenberg im Kanton Zug* independently discusses the comparison with the Alsatian Hunenburg arms. Its text distinguishes two outward-turned swan necks in the shield from two more, facing heraldic left, as crest. It cautions that the proposed comparison with the Swiss Hünenberg family does not hold up on first review. Do not conflate the Alsatian Huneburg with the Swiss Hünenberg.

Sources:

- [Jahrbuch der K.-K. Heraldischen Gesellschaft Adler, searchable digitized text](https://knihovna.archives.cz/arl-zao/en/csg/?key=2059401912&repo=zaorepo) — passage under the Huneburg entry says “oben zwei abgewendete silberne Schwanenhälse mit rothen Schnäbeln,” then explains that older seals had the swan necks only as crest and that they were later taken into the shield.
- [Gabi Meier Mohamed, *Burgruine Hünenberg im Kanton Zug* (2020), digitized PDF](https://www.zg.ch/behoerden/direktion-des-innern/amt-fuer-denkmalpflege-und-archaeologie/publikationen/ftw-simplelayout-filelistingblock-1/burgruine_huenenberg_im_kanton_zug_sbkam_48_reduziert.pdf/%40%40download/file) — discussion of the Alsatian Hunenburg arms; the book metadata and author are corroborated by [Zug Kultur’s review](https://www.zugkultur.ch/artikel/diese-ritter-haben-mehr-als-einen-fussabdruck-hinterlassen).

This is the strongest new non-Rosenberg family lead found in the text search. It is a historical armorial description, but I have not independently inspected the relevant medieval seals or a colored armorial image in this search.

## Other historical-family lead: Lords of Mattstete (Thuringia)

The Mattstedt entry reports that the Lords of Mattstete had two swan necks turned away from one another in the shield, with a further single swan neck above as crest. It dates the arms to the 12th/13th century. The same article identifies members of the family under spellings Matstat, Mattstedt, Mattstete and Matstetten and cites Werner Eckart’s local history, “Kleine Ortschronik von Mattstedt,” *Apoldaer Heimat* 3 (1985), pp. 19–22, as a source for the family history. The blazon sentence itself is not given a separate citation there, so treat the date and charge as a promising lead needing confirmation in the cited local history or a contemporary seal.

Sources:

- [Mattstedt, German Wikipedia, “Wappen” section and references](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mattstedt) — states: “Das Wappen der Herren von Mattstete zeigte bereits im 12./13. Jahrhundert 2 sich abgewendete Schwanenhälse im Schild … darüber ein weiterer … Schwanenhals.”
- [Wikimedia Commons category: swan heads in heraldry](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Swan_heads_in_heraldry) — contains contemporary vector images for several modern arms, but no independently verified medieval image of the Mattstete arms was established here.

## Swiss municipal arms preserving older family arms: Mattstetten

The municipality of Mattstetten (Canton Bern) has two silver swan torsos/necks turned away from one another on red. The municipality’s own history page says this is the arms of the medieval Knights of Mattstetten, that the lineage died out by the end of the Middle Ages, and that the arms survived as seals on documents before being revived in 1935. This is a strong reported historical-family match; the municipal page does not supply dates or photographs of the medieval seals, so those should be checked before claiming a particular seal is visually confirmed.

Sources:

- [Official Mattstetten municipal history](https://www.mattstetten.ch/de/gemeinde/index.php) — blazon and statement that the arms belong to the Knights of Mattstetten and were preserved on seals.
- [Wikimedia Commons, Mattstetten arms](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mattstetten-coat_of_arms.svg) — gives the blazon “In Rot zwei schwebende, abgewendete silberne Schwanenrümpfe mit goldenen Schnäbeln” and cites the Bern cantonal armorial (1981), p. 182/183.

This is not evidence that the Thuringian Mattstete and Bernese Mattstetten families are related; the similar place/family names should not be merged without genealogical evidence.

## Modern derivative: Oberhünigen / Niederhünigen, Canton Bern

Oberhünigen’s modern municipal arms use two outward-facing swan necks; a contemporary local article says the current design is based on the older arms of Oberhünigen. Niederhünigen adopted the motif from Oberhünigen, rendered as two outward-facing silver swan necks on red below a silver chief with a red star. Wikimedia Commons records a 1943 adoption date for Niederhünigen and cites the 1981 Bern cantonal armorial.

Sources:

- [Niederhünigen arms, Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:CHE_Niederh%C3%BCnigen_COA.svg) — blazon explicitly says “zwei abgewendete silberne Schwanenhälse”; gives 1943 and source citation to the 1981 Bern armorial.
- [Local report on Oberhünigen’s arms](https://www.bern-ost.ch/Oberhuenigen-Ein-neues-Logo-muss-her-693634) — describes the municipality’s two outward-facing swan necks and identifies the current arms as the basis for the logo.
- [Niederhünigen explanatory account](https://www.crwflags.com/Fotw/flags/ch-be195.html) — blazons two swan heads addorsed and says the motif was taken from Oberhünigen’s flag.

These establish additional visible arms with the required orientation but are municipal survivals/adaptations, not independent medieval armorial families on the evidence currently located.

## Similar-looking textual hits excluded

- **Niebelschitz and Molsdorf Weller genannt**, in an online ordinary of Siebmacher’s 1605 *Wappenbuch*, are blazoned “two swan’s heads respectant” (Molsdorf also holds a ring). *Respectant* means they face each other, so these do not match the outward-facing criterion. [Uckelman, “An Ordinary of Siebmacher’s Wappenbuch,” bird-head section](https://www.ellipsis.cx/~liana/heraldry/siebmacher/birdhead.html).
- The same distinction matters for arms described as *zugewendet* or *einwärts sehend*: those face inward and should not be counted.
- Searches also surfaced many paired swan heads in crests (including Behr variants and Rosenberg/Uissigheim crests); these were excluded unless the text explicitly placed the pair in the shield.

## Terminology and search notes

Useful German forms were *zwei abgewendete Schwanenhälse*, *voneinander abgewendete Schwanenhälse*, *im Schild*, and *oben im Schild*. English searches included “two swan heads addorsed,” “two swan necks addorsed,” and “swan heads facing away.” French queries included *deux cols de cygne* and *affrontés/adossés* variants; the hits found did not establish an additional qualifying historical shield. The term *Schwanenrumpf* can describe a swan’s upper body including neck and head, as in Mattstetten.

## Assessment

The internet description search does find more than the project’s listed manuscript pages: Huneburg (Alsace) is a strong historical-family textual match, while Mattstete (Thuringia) is another promising but less securely sourced medieval report. Mattstetten (Bern) preserves a comparable historical family device in municipal arms and says it survived on seals. Oberhünigen/Niederhünigen add modern municipal examples derived from older local arms. None of these should be presented as a newly image-verified medieval object based on text search alone. The Huneburg text is especially valuable because it explicitly warns that the charge moved from crest to shield over time, so the date of a particular seal or armorial depiction matters.

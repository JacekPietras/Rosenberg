# Two swan necks in Franconian heraldry: Rosenberg and Uissigheim seal evidence

## Findings

The recognizable Rosenberg/Uissigheim device is a divided, red-and-silver (or red-and-white) shield, usually described as divided both horizontally and vertically and showing three alternating red/silver bars or rows of rectangular panels, with a helmet crest of two outward-facing swan necks. It is a crest above the shield, not usually a pair of swan heads charged on a blue shield. The blue-ground clue may come from unrelated arms with swan necks (for example the municipal arms of Zwingenberg, adopted in 1913), or from a colored later depiction; it does not match the standard Rosenberg/Uissigheim blazon.

The strongest primary evidence available in the project is archival catalogued medieval charters listing sealers, with image scans linked below. These establish that Rosenberg and Uissigheim bearers sealed documents in the relevant period; they do not by themselves establish every heraldic detail unless the seal image is inspected legibly. Contemporary monument evidence independently records the same crest on funerary sculpture. Later local histories name the other families said to share it, but the proposed common descent is a hypothesis, not established merely by armorial similarity.

## Funerary and monument evidence

Uissigheim's published local history page quotes Florian Werr's 1910 description of Arnold's tomb slab: shield and helmet crest are visible; the two swan necks are laid sideways because of the confined space. Werr also reports the same shield and helmet with two necks on a stone set into the south aisle wall at Bronnbach, plus a broken grave slab in the east cloister. The page reproduces Werr's account and also quotes the later 1966 interpretation by Helmut Lauf. These are descriptions of physical monuments, but the online page is a modern transcription; it is not a substitute for examination of the stones or their photographs. [Uissigheim, “Ritter Arnold,” esp. description quoting Werr](https://uissigheim.de/geschichte/ritter-arnold/).

The project contains a photo file named [`Uissigheim_grabplate_1332.png`](../../data/notes/img/Uissigheim_grabplate_1332.png) and a note describing the slab as low-relief shield, helmet, and crest. That image is a useful local comparison. A 19th-century antiquarian report in *Zeitschrift für die Geschichte des Oberrheins* likewise describes a Rosenberg grave monument with “zwei Schwanenhälse” in the center and the counterchanged red-and-white panelled Rosenberg shield; the scan is discoverable at [ZGO, vol. 46 (1892), digitized PDF](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/64/Zeitschrift_f%C3%BCr_die_Geschichte_des_Oberrheins._Bd._046%2C_1892_%28IA_zeitschriftfurdi4618unse%29.pdf). As a 19th-century report, this is later documentation of a monument, not medieval testimony in its own right.

## How widespread? Related families and limits

The Uissigheim local-history summary says the same arms occur with von Rosenberg, von Höchheim, von Thalheim, von Rannenberg (also Kannenberg), von Randersacker, von Reinstein, and von Rieneck, and suggests these houses came from a common root. [Uissigheim history page](https://uissigheim.de/geschichte/). This is a lead list, not a demonstrated genealogy: each name needs confirmation from its own seal, tomb, or dated armorial evidence. In the 1328 Bronnbach charter, “Eberhard called von Rannenberg in Uissigheim” is directly present among the sealers, which is especially valuable evidence that Rannenberg and Uissigheim identities overlap in this setting.

The digitized **Scheibler Armorial** is a useful late-medieval/early-modern visual control for Franconian arms. Its index gives the Franconian “von Roßenberg” shield as paly red and silver in two counterchanged horizontal divisions, consistent with the Rosenberg shield; the index does not itself state the swan-neck crest in this entry. [Scheibler Armorial index, entry 235](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Scheibler_Armorial?uselang=en#mw-content-text). The original manuscript image collection is linked from that page. Use manuscript plates, rather than the modern index's crosswalk, to test other family attributions.

There are genuine blue-field swan-neck arms elsewhere in the wider region: Zwingenberg's municipal arms are described as blue with three (2:1) silver swan necks with gold beaks, based on a 19th-century seal and adopted in 1913. That is a distinct design and date, not evidence for the medieval Rosenberg/Uissigheim family arms. [Neckar-Odenwald-Kreis arms list](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liste_der_Wappen_im_Neckar-Odenwald-Kreis#Zwingenberg).

## Working conclusions / next checks

1. Describe the Rosenberg/Uissigheim motif as **two outward-facing (abgewendet) swan necks as a helmet crest**, over a counterchanged red/silver barred or panelled shield. Avoid “blue field” unless a specific colored source is supplied.
2. Directly inspect the 1314 and 1328 seal images at high resolution and match each impression to its named sealer before asserting individual blazons. The 1314 project image annotation suggests the surviving Arnold seal is counterpaly; the catalog itself only identifies sealers and attachment state.
3. Treat Höchheim, Thalheim, Randersacker, Reinstein, and Rieneck as promising comparative candidates, not proven kinsmen. Prioritize their original seals and contemporaneous tomb/epitaph images; then compare shared shield pattern and crest separately.
4. The tomb evidence is promising: Arnold's slab at Uissigheim and the Bronnbach wall monument offer independent material witnesses to the two-neck crest. Obtain/inspect photographs and establish date/identity before treating the modern transcriptions as a definitive visual reading.

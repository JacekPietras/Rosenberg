# Swan-neck crests on Franconian monuments and armorial pages

## Main result

The Rosenberg/Uissigheim motif is best documented as a **helmet crest of two swan necks**, above a red-and-silver panelled shield. It is not, in the medieval examples located here, a pair of heads charged on a blue shield. The armorial evidence and funerary evidence should be kept distinct: late-medieval armorials give a color reference, while most surviving tomb sculpture is monochrome and cannot establish tinctures by itself.

## Armorial evidence

* **Scheibler Armorial, Rosenberg entry (entry/page 235 in the image index).** The Bayerische Staatsbibliothek manuscript is Cod.icon. 312 c; the BSB dates the digitized manuscript broadly from the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries. The Rosenberg plate is directly viewable as a scan/reproduction at [Wikimedia Commons, full image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rosenberg-Scheibler235ps.jpg); its source attribution points to the [BSB manuscript record/viewer](https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/de/view/bsb00007174). The image shows the counterchanged red/silver panelled shield and the helmet crest with a pair of opposed swan-neck forms. This is an armorial image, not a monument, and provides a useful colored comparison for sculpted crests. The manuscript image label/attribution identifies Rosenberg; the precise dating of this particular leaf is not independently established by the Commons metadata.
* **BSB Cod.icon. 307, image 00573, manuscript page 617.** The BSB viewer identifies the volume as *Sammlung von Wappen aus verschiedenen, besonders deutschen Ländern*, dated around 1600; the catalogue record describes 368 leaves and a broad collection of German, Austrian, French, Spanish, and other arms. The [page-specific viewer](https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/en/view/bsb00020245?page=573) and [direct IIIF image](https://api.digitale-sammlungen.de/iiif/image/v3/bsb00020245_00573/full/max/0/default.jpg) show handwritten “617” at upper right, corresponding to digital image/canvas 573. It is therefore safer to cite this as image 00573 / manuscript page 617, rather than “folio 573.” On the right side of the top row, the gold caption below the shield reads **“Stachel”** (the last letters are joined but the reading is clear); a separate gold word above the crest is crossed through and not confidently legible. The shield below that caption contains two opposed, long-necked bird heads/necks; the crest repeats a pair of similar forms. This is strong evidence for a Stachel-labeled paired-neck device in this c.1600 armorial, but the page is an uncolored ink sketch, so it cannot establish a blue field or any tinctures. The nearby page entries are separate arms arranged in rows and columns; the adjacent labels include “Gernberg” to the left, while no connecting notation establishes kinship. [Bavarikon object record and page viewer](https://www.bavarikon.de/object/bav:BSB-HSS-00000BSB00020245?lang=en&p=573).
* **BSB Cod.icon. 309, earlier colored armorial.** The BSB viewer is [available here](https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/en/view/bsb00002106); the work is titled *Wappen besonders von deutschen Geschlechtern*. The BSB catalog and manuscript viewer describe a German armorial conventionally dated 1475–1560, and the IIIF manifest contains 169 scanned canvases. This matches the general description of an earlier colored witness, but no folio/page number or exact image URL accompanied the reference available for this report. I therefore cannot securely match a particular Cod.icon.309 leaf to the described blue shield with two swan-neck figures, or verify its caption and neighboring entries. Treat that image as a promising visual comparison only until its leaf is identified; do not use the color or attribution as a proven family blazon on the basis of the Cod.icon.307 sketch.

The Rosenberg family blazon is independently represented in Scheibler as a counterchanged red/silver panelled shield with a two-neck crest. Cod.icon.307 adds a distinct Stachel-labeled example where paired necks occur on the shield as well as in the crest, but its monochrome sketch leaves the color unresolved. A blue field is only supported if the cited Cod.icon.309 scan can be tied to its exact folio and caption.

## Funerary and monument evidence

* **Konrad von Rosenberg and Adelheid von Hirschhorn, grave monument described in 1892.** A report in *Zeitschrift für die Geschichte des Oberrheins*, vol. 46 (1892), describes a 1.36 × 2.04 m stone, then kept in the cellar of a mason’s business. The report says the center of the monument showed the Rosenberg helmet crest—“zwei Schwanenhälse”—and identifies Rosenberg and Hirschhorn arms in its four quarters. It describes the Rosenberg shield as counterchanged red and white panels. This is unusually explicit evidence connecting the two-neck crest and the shield on a funerary monument, but it is a nineteenth-century description of a monument, not a modern photograph or medieval catalog record. [Digitized volume PDF](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/64/Zeitschrift_f%C3%BCr_die_Geschichte_des_Oberrheins._Bd._046%2C_1892_%28IA_zeitschriftfurdi4618unse%29.pdf) (search within the scan for “Konrad von Rosenberg” or “zwei Schwanenhälse”). The report’s excerpt does not make the monument’s original location clear, so location should not be inferred from the storage detail.
* **Rosenberg effigies at Wölchingen (Boxberg), 1447 and 1449.** The Frankendom/evangelical Johanniterkirche has life-size funerary reliefs for Arnold von Rosenberg (†1447) and Eberhard X von Rosenberg (†1449). [Regional description of the church and monuments](https://www.pro-region.de/de/proregion/dieregion/staedte_und_gemeinden/Boxberg_Kirchen_Frankendom.php). A substantial, recent photo set of the individual monuments is available in [Wikimedia Commons’ Boxberg photographs, 15 May 2026](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Boxberg_(Baden)_photographs_taken_on_2026-05-15): files are titled “Epitaph Arnold von Rosenberg (gest. 1447)” and “Epitaph Eberhard X von Rosenberg (gest. 1449).” The source establishes date, identity, and viewable photographic coverage. I have not found an authoritative description that specifically identifies two swan necks on either relief; include them as close candidates for image inspection, not yet confirmed two-neck examples.
* **Konrad von Rosenberg at Gnötzheim, died 1596.** The municipal church history describes his life-size effigy in armor, the funerary monument, and seven lineage arms above it in St. Johannis. It also notes a separate Rosenberg tomb slab for Lienhardt (†1517). [Municipality of Martinsheim, church description](https://www.martinsheim.de/kultur-freizeit/geschichte/chronik-gnoetzheim.html). The account confirms an armorial monument but does not identify which of the seven arms bears the two-neck crest; treat it as a strong site lead, not direct verification of the swans.

## What the evidence does and does not show

The clearest color-and-crest witness found in this angle is the Scheibler Rosenberg plate. Cod.icon.307 is a separate, c.1600 Stachel-labeled sketch with paired necks on the shield and crest; it does not preserve color. The reported blue field from Cod.icon.309 remains plausible but unverified at page level because its exact leaf has not been identified. The most explicit funerary description is the 1892 account of Konrad and Adelheid’s monument, which names the two-neck helmet crest and counterchanged shield together. The Wölchingen and Gnötzheim funerary monuments provide further primary objects with accessible location descriptions and photographs, but their swan-neck detail still needs close visual confirmation.

No image-verified funerary or armorial example from the other families named in the Uissigheim local-history list (Höchheim, Thalheim, Rannenberg/Kannenberg, Randersacker, Reinstein, or Rieneck) was established in this pass. That list is useful for follow-up, but the shared descent it proposes is not proven by the monument evidence reviewed here. Likewise, no evidence in these monuments supports a blue field for Rosenberg/Uissigheim; a blue background in an individual colored image may instead be the ground of the illustration or an unrelated armorial design.

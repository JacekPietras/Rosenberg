# Zwingenberg in 1337–39: focused charter and seal search

## Finding

The 1338 date does correspond to two actual Zwingenberg instruments, not merely a later event summary. Georg Heinrich Krieg von Hochfelden printed both texts in *Die Veste Zwingenberg am Neckar* (1843), printed pp. 123–124 (book’s internal page numbers 123–124). He says the first is transcribed “nach dem Original”; the second from the Kurpfälzisches Copial-Buch at the Großherzogliches General-Landesarchiv Karlsruhe. These are the best located primary-source witnesses, but neither document text describes a swan, and Krieg reproduces no seal images. The first original’s present shelfmark and current survival have not been established. The accessible 1843 scan is a print transcript, not a facsimile of either charter.

The first instrument, dated Thursday after St Lawrence, 1338 (13 August), is a sworn undertaking by the brothers Wilhelm and Dietherich von Zwingenberg and their nephew Wiprecht. It bars them from harboring or maintaining people acting against Count Ulrich of Württemberg while a pact among Württemberg, Mainz, and the Palatine counts lasts. The edition says all three attached their own seals. The next-day instrument (14 August), in the edition from the Karlsruhe palatine cartulary, is by Dietrich and Wiprecht for themselves and the co-holders of Zwingenberg: they swear to serve and obey Archbishop Heinrich of Mainz and the two Counts Palatine Ruprecht, with the obligation surviving the death of one or two of the princes but ending with all three. The edition says their seals were attached. Neither is a blazon or specifically an armorial grant.

## The 1338 date and what it does—and does not—document

The event chronology in LEO-BW and modern summaries compresses distinct arrangements. The first 1338 letter is about not sheltering enemies of Württemberg; the second swears service to Mainz and the Palatines. Krieg’s narrative presents them as instruments in the broader Neckar shipping/conflict context. The source trail proves that an original of the first was seen by the 1843 editor, but it does not by itself prove that the original survives today. The shelfmark is missing from the edition. The second is explicitly witnessed through a copybook, not a named original.

The scholarly chronology is more exact. The 30 November 1339 truce is REM 4454 / *Urkunden-Regesten zur Tätigkeit des deutschen Königs- und Hofgerichts* (UR) V, no. 351. The inquiry result dated 8 January 1340 is REM 4471 / Würdtwein V, no. 87. Later proceedings include REM 4455 (UR V, no. 352; Weech, *60 Urkunden*, no. 38) and May–June 1340 arbitration records. These are useful leads to the charter dossier, but they postdate 1338 and concern jurisdiction and castle rights, not heraldry.

- [Würdtwein, *Nova subsidia diplomatica*, vol. V, digitized volume entry](https://www.e-rara.ch/zuz/content/titleinfo/7030879)

The 1843 edition gives a more detailed source trail for subsequent instruments. Its 1340 arbitration commission and the opposing Mainz and Palatine decisions are printed from the Kurpfälzisches Copial-Buch; the text of the Palatine decision says the three arbitrators issued it under their own seals. The edition also prints the decision of 8 January 1340 from Würdtwein V, no. 87. Modern research identifies that item as REM 4471: Rudolf, count of Wertheim, certified the inquiry into whether the castlemen were Mainz ministerials. It is a narrative record of testimony, not an heraldic description. The transcribed witnesses and claims do not identify the heraldic device on any seal. No original shelfmark or direct charter/seal image for REM 4471 has yet been established.

The principal 1339–40 sequence in the modern study is: imperial truce 30 November 1339 (REM 4454; UR V, no. 351); related proceedings REM 4455 (UR V, no. 352; Weech, *60 Urkunden*, no. 38); Rudolf of Wertheim’s inquiry report 8 January 1340 (REM 4471; Würdtwein V, no. 87); a May arbitration and divergent judgments on 26 June 1340 (REM 4536–4538, Krieg nos. 4–6); and the imperial confirmation on 1 September 1340 (REM 4581; UR V, no. 366; Gudenus, *Codex diplomaticus anecdotorum*, III, p. 314, no. 226). The published chronology does not turn these into evidence for swans; they concern feudal status, castle access, and the Fürstenstein dispute.

## Heraldic evidence that should not be mistaken for the 1338 object

Fritz Liebig’s 1958 article says that Wilhelm von Wimpfen’s frequently used seal showed “das Zwingenberger Wappen mit dem Reiherhals” (the Zwingenberg arms with a heron neck). The article does not illustrate that seal at the cited passage. The corresponding Sigilla entry for Wilhelm’s 1240 seal describes a single heron neck and head, not two swan heads. This is an earlier heraldic lead, not an image of a 1338 Zwingenberg seal.

- [Liebig, “Zur Geschichte des mittleren Neckartals …,” *Badische Heimat* 38 (1958), PDF](https://regionalia.blb-karlsruhe.de/frontdoor/deliver/index/docId/26164/file/BLB_Badische_Heimat_1958_2.pdf)
- [Sigilla record for Wilhelm von Wimpfen, seal dated 1240](https://sigilla.irht.cnrs.fr/11189)
- [Direct seal image in Sigilla](https://sigilla.irht.cnrs.fr/iiris_img/private/image/FRAD067_0151NUM0107_03.png)

The classification “heron” is material: it should not be silently normalized to “swan.” Even if the later municipal arms of Neckar Zwingenberg are described as three swan necks, the early seal identified by Liebig/Sigilla is one bird neck, and the 1338–40 dispute documents located here do not supply evidence of a two-swan shield.

### A later family-seal description in the same 1843 edition

Krieg’s appendix does provide a useful, but later, comparison. The 1368 sale charter by Hans, Wilhelm, Beringer and Wiprecht von Zwingenberg (also called von Berlichingen) is dated in its text as Tuesday before Corpus Christi; this resolves to 6 June 1368 in the Julian calendar (14 June proleptic Gregorian). The four brothers sell dependent people and five properties at Neukirchen to Elisabeth, widow of Engelhart von Hirschhorn. Krieg states that their seals are attached and adds: “Die Zwingenbergischen Siegel sind angehängt; alle haben im Schilde den Schwanenhals mit einer Umschrift, die aber nicht mehr leserlich ist” (“The Zwingenberg seals are attached; all have the swan neck in the shield, with an inscription that is no longer legible”). This is direct editorial evidence that the seals were seen on the charter and that the shield charge on each was identified as a swan neck. The singular phrase gives no count of necks/heads per seal and Krieg reproduces no seal drawing; nor does the appendix provide an archival shelfmark for this charter. This later evidence supports the family’s swan-neck shield but cannot by itself identify the devices on the three seals attached to the 13 August 1338 charter. The 1338 source chain remains visually unclosed.

#### 1368 charter: focused archival and image follow-up

The 1368 charter is printed as Beilage 3 in the 1843 edition, pp. 145–146. Krieg explicitly labels the transcription “Nach dem Original” on p. 145. On p. 146 the instrument dates itself as Tuesday before Corpus Christi in 1368, corresponding to 6 June 1368 in the Julian calendar (14 June proleptic Gregorian). The grantors are four brothers, Hans (Hanntz), Wilhelm, Beringer, and Wiprecht of Zwingenberg, also called of Berlichingen; the recipient is Elisabeth, widow of the late Engelhard of Hirschhorn. It conveys dependent people and five properties at Neukirchen. Krieg says their seals are attached and his editorial parenthesis says every one has “den Schwanenhals” (singular) in the shield. This strongly supports one swan-neck charge per shield on each of the four seals, although the text does not separately enumerate the four impressions or describe their exact shapes.

The reproduction contains only the printed transcription; Krieg’s volume does not illustrate the seal impressions. Searching web-indexed Sigilla, Landesarchiv Baden-Württemberg, Arcinsys/Hessisches Staatsarchiv, and archive portal records by the parties, place-name variants, date wording, and the quoted seal phrase did not locate a current original shelfmark or a secondary seal photograph/drawing. The 1843 statement “Nach dem Original” proves that Krieg had access to an original in 1843, but it does not identify where he consulted it or establish its current repository.

#### Provenance trace and related records checked

Krieg’s preface gives a general provenance context, not an item-level citation: he says the Baden margraves Wilhelm and Maximilian owned the Zwingenberg lordship, and that Markgraf Wilhelm collected the documents and sources underlying the book. The preface does not name a repository, archive series, or shelfmark for Beilage 3. The later *Schlossarchiv Zwingenberg* holdings at the Generallandesarchiv Karlsruhe are therefore a plausible institutional lead, but not a demonstrated home of this medieval original. The current finding aids describe two subfonds, “Altes Reich” (69 Baden, Zwingenberg-1) and “Badische Verwaltung” (69 Baden, Zwingenberg-2); the latter is described in the Landesarchiv’s account as administrative records. Neither online aid exposes an item matching the 1368 transaction, and the Schlossarchiv account does not establish that the 1368 charter entered those later administrative holdings. [Krieg’s digitized book](https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/en/view/bsb11246756) · [Landesarchiv account of the Schlossarchiv and its current finding aids](https://www.landesarchiv-bw.de/sixcms/media.php/120/AN70_Gesamt_Web.pdf).

The same 1843 volume gives a related transaction by the four brothers in 1360: Hans, Wilhelm, Wiprecht, and Beringer, also styled von Berlichingen, sell a quarter of Zwingenberg to Heinrich von Erligheim. Krieg cites the Kur-Pfälzisches Copial-Buch for this text and says the brothers’ and their cousins’ seals were named in the instrument; he does not describe the devices or reproduce the impressions. This is a useful archival name/variant lead, but not a located original or a swan image. The cited 1357 agreement likewise appears as a Kur-Pfälzisches Copial-Buch witness and names the family group, not the 1368 sale’s current archival location.

Dagmar Kraus’s *Archiv der Freiherren von Berlichingen: Jagsthausen. Urkundenregesten 1244–1860* (1999; ISBN 978-3-17-015372-1) is a printed, 496-page inventory, not a digitized/searchable source in the catalogs checked. The Landesarchiv confirms it is orderable. Because the 1368 grantors are also called von Berlichingen, this inventory remains a reasonable lead to consult, but no exact entry or shelfmark has been confirmed there. [Landesarchiv publication record](https://www.landesarchiv-bw.de/de/landesarchiv/publikationen/inventare-der-nichtstaatlichen-archive/45718) · [review reporting the inventory’s scope and 586 regesta](https://www.archive.nrw.de/sites/default/files/media/files/Archivar_2004-1.pdf).

The present result is therefore unchanged on the central point: the seal devices are attested in Krieg’s editorial description of the 1368 original, but no physical original, archive shelfmark, or photograph of any of the four seals has been located. Do not treat the Jagsthausen Berlichingen archive or GLAK 69 Baden, Zwingenberg as confirmed repositories without checking their unindexed materials or the printed Kraus inventory.

One potential but unconfirmed related witness is the digitized Hirschhorn lordship cartulary, HStAD, C 1 A, 16 (former identifier C 1 Nr. 5), catalogued as a 1415–1503 copybook of 240 documents and available as a digital viewer. Its online catalog record’s listed examples do not identify this 1368 sale, and I have not verified a copy of it in the manuscript. It must not be cited as the source for the 1368 text without checking the contents/register. [Arcinsys record and digital viewer](https://arcinsys.hessen.de/arcinsys/showArchivalDescriptionDetails?archivalDescriptionId=4844196).

**Assessment:** high confidence that a four-seal 1368 original existed and that Krieg identified a swan neck in each shield; no seal image is currently located. This 1368 evidence is distinct from the separate 1338 originals/copybook texts, whose seal devices remain undescribed.

The printed 1338 appendix pages have been visually inspected: the first charter is on Internet Archive scan leaf `n146` (printed p. 123), the second on `n147` (printed p. 124). The prior `n144`/`n145` links point to unnumbered/other preliminary leaves, not the charters; use these corrected direct image links: [p. 123](https://archive.org/download/11246756bsb/page/n146_w1024.jpg) · [p. 124](https://archive.org/download/11246756bsb/page/n147_w1024.jpg). The 1843 volume has architectural/landscape illustrations, but no reproduction or drawing of these 1338 seals was found. The added 1368 verbal note is the only seal-device description identified in the volume relevant to the Zwingenberg family.

## Place and name disambiguation

- **Zwingenberg am Neckar:** the relevant castle and 1338 opening-right context; earliest local entry cited by LEO-BW is *Tuingenberc* in 1253.
- **Zwingenberg an der Bergstraße:** a separate settlement and lordship. Its known historical city arms use the Katzenelnbogen lion and hearts/sea-leaves, not swan heads. No relevant 1337–39 swan-bearing seal or monument surfaced in this search.
- **Kochendorfer Zwingenberg (Bad Friedrichshall):** a different castle/place-name associated in LEO-BW with Wimpfen/Kochendorf ministerials. The kinship and heraldic history merits separate source-level checking; it is not evidence for the 1338 Neckar-castle document.
- **Franconian people named Zwingenberg:** a Würzburg university castle database records brothers Wiprecht, Wilhelm, Wicker and Dietrich Zwingenberg as officials at Wildenberg in 1332. This is a different, regionally relevant namesake lead, but it provides no 1337–39 two-swan object or image.

Sources: [LEO-BW on Neckar Zwingenberg](https://www.leo-bw.de/en-GB/detail-gis/-/Detail/details/ORT/labw_ortslexikon/6203/Zwingenberg), [LEO-BW on Kochendorf](https://www.leo-bw.de/fr/detail-gis/-/Detail/details/ORT/labw_ortslexikon/1800/Kochendorf%2B-%2BAltgemeinde~Teilort), [Historisches Unterfranken, Wildenberg record](https://www.historisches-unterfranken.uni-wuerzburg.de/burgen/burgen-einzeln.php?burg=146).

## Search result and remaining archival target

Searches covered `Zwingenberg`, `Zwingenberc`, `Twingenberg`, `Tuingenberc`, `Zwingenburg`, combined with 1337–40, `Siegel`, `Urkunde`, `Schwan`, `Schwanenhals`, `Schwanenkopf`, and `Reiherhals`; and web-indexed Sigilla, LEO-BW, Regesta Imperii citations, Würdtwein, Krieg von Hochfelden’s digitized book, and the Würzburg historical castle database. This located the two printed 1338 primary-text witnesses and their stated transmission, but no visual image of their seal impressions. No inspected 1338–40 charter text names a swan charge.

The next archival task is to identify the 1843 editor’s “Original” first letter in a surviving charter series or house archive and the exact shelfmark/folio of the Karlsruhe Kurpfälzisches Copial-Buch witness. Searches of web-indexed GLA catalogue material and digitized seal-catalogue entries located no 13 August 1338 item by these names, no attached-seal image, and no published device description for the three seals. The online GLA Schlossarchiv Zwingenberg finding aids located in this pass describe much later administrative material, not a medieval charter; they therefore do not establish loss of the original. Nor does the vague 1843 citation for the copybook give enough to identify a codex: a separate nearby reference in the same book to a Pfälzisches Copialbuch No. 19, Litt. III, fol. 264b concerns a different document and must not be assigned to the 1338 text without a matching entry. The conclusion supported is: **the 13 August 1338 original was seen by the editor and had three attached personal seals; the 14 August text survives in a Karlsruhe copybook witness; neither 1338 seal set is presently represented by a located image or blazon. A later 1368 charter is described as bearing Zwingenberg seals with a swan neck in each shield.** Keep these distinct from the 1240 single heron neck and from any claim of two swan heads on the 1338 shield.

## Follow-up audit: parties, transmission, and the seal question

The 1843 scan and OCR were rechecked against each other (printed pp. 123–124; Internet Archive leaves `n146`/`n147`). The first text names “Wilhelm vnde Dietherich von Zwingenberg gebruder” and “wiprecht vnſers Bruder Sun,” i.e. Wilhelm and Dietherich, brothers, and Wiprecht, their brother’s son. It says all three “henken wir alle drie” their own seals to the letter. The date formula is Thursday after St Lawrence in 1338, 13 August. This is a contemporaneous family relationship stated in the instrument, not a modern genealogical reconstruction.

The next-day text calls its issuers Dieterich of Twingenberg and Wiprecht, “seines Bruders Sohn Herrn Wiprecht Seligen,” and says it is made for themselves and all co-holders of the castle. This independently confirms the uncle/nephew relationship and suggests the two texts concern the same generation of the Neckar castle-holding family. The 1843 editor attributes this text to a Kur-Pfälzisches Copial-Buch at the Karlsruhe General State Archive, but gives no volume, folio, or modern signature at this passage. It is a copy witness; it cannot supply the missing seal images of the 13 August original.

**Family identification:** these are the Edlen/Herren von Zwingenberg of the Neckar castle involved in the Mainz–Palatinate opening-right and Fürstenstein dispute, not Zwingenberg am Bergstraße. The 1843 narrative and modern scholarly treatment place the family in that conflict; the text itself identifies them by the castle and their co-holding. I have not found a securely sourced personal-name equivalence that would let us assign Wilhelm, Dietherich, or the younger Wiprecht to a particular nickname or later Berlichingen identity. The 1368 item is a separate, later instrument: Krieg explicitly says those four brothers were also called von Berlichingen and describes a swan neck on each shield. That cannot be retrojected onto these 1338 seals without images or an explicit blazon.

The web-indexed search was repeated against the exact name forms and phrases from the transcriptions, Landesarchiv Baden-Württemberg catalogue pages, Sigilla, LEO-BW, DDB, and secondary literature. It still produced no present-day shelfmark for the 13 August original, no catalogued seal impression for either charter, and no iconographic description of their seals. Krieg’s “Nach dem Original” establishes that an original parchment was available to him in 1843, but does not establish that it survives. The next-day copybook attribution proves the text’s archival transmission, but the citation is too imprecise to identify the codex. No image URL for the 1338 seals can therefore be supplied at present.

These are printed text pages, not seal photographs. The contemporary dispute context is independently discussed in [Königliche Gerichtsbarkeit und regionale Konfliktbeilegung im deutschen Spätmittelalter, pp. 198–200](https://dokumen.pub/knigliche-gerichtsbarkeit-und-regionale-konfliktbeilegung-im-deutschen-sptmittelalter-die-regierungszeit-ludwigs-des-bayern-13141347-abt-b-abhandlungen-zur-deutschen-rechtsgeschichte-1nbsped-9783428545445-9783428145447.html); its cited 1339–40 records concern the castle dispute and ministerial status, not the arms.

# Hüneburg (Alsace): paired swan necks in shield and crest

## Finding

The relevant family is the **Alsatian lords / marshals of Hüneburg (Huneburg, Hunenburg; French Hunebourg)**, whose seat was the castle Huneburg near Neuwiller-lès-Saverne (Bas-Rhin). They are also called Marshals of Strasbourg because they held the hereditary great marshalship of the Strasbourg bishopric. They are not the Swiss lords of Hünenberg (Zug). The latter have a different heraldic tradition; a recent archaeological study explicitly discusses and rejects the old proposed descent from the Alsatian family, despite the coincidental swan-neck crest resemblance ([Meier, *Burgruine Hünenberg im Kanton Zug*, section 3.3](https://www.zg.ch/behoerden/direktion-des-innern/amt-fuer-denkmalpflege-und-archaeologie/publikationen/ftw-simplelayout-filelistingblock-1/burgruine_huenenberg_im_kanton_zug_sbkam_48_reduziert.pdf)).

**The early Hüneburg evidence places the paired swan necks in the crest, not in the shield.** This is now supported by an image-verified seal impression used in 1248 and a near-contemporary manuscript depiction made around/after 1391. The shield in the manuscript has one swan head in chief; the crest carries two outward-facing swan necks. A later heraldic tradition moves the pair into the shield. The change is credible in outline, but its date remains unproved: the earliest located representation with the pair in the shield is Schöpflin's 1761 printed plate, known here through a modern scholarly description rather than direct visual inspection of the plate.

This is therefore a meaningful parallel for the two-swan-neck motif, especially as a crest, but **not yet a secure medieval match for two outward-facing swan necks as shield charges**. Do not count the Hüneburg family as a verified paired-charge-on-shield example without the primary image or an independently dated seal showing it.

## Family identity and names

The pertinent group is called *Marschalken von Huneburg*, *Marschalken von Straßburg*, or simply the lords of Huneburg/Hunebourg. The family held the castle at Huneburg (Petite-Pierre / La Petite-Pierre vicinity, Alsace) and the Strasbourg episcopal marshal office. The Grünenberg armorial entry is explicitly captioned “marschalken von hiuremberg stiffter zum grünemverd” (marshals of Hüneburg, founders of Grünenwerd). The *Ordinary of Medieval Armorials* identifies this as the Alsatian Marschalk von Huneburg / Marschalk von Strasbourg and cites Kindler von Knobloch, *Oberbadisches Geschlechterbuch* 3:33 and Siebmacher A 260 ([Grünenberg armorial index](https://www.armorial.dk/german/Grunenberg.pdf); [ordinary entry](https://www.armorial.dk/ordinary/families20.pdf)).

The later history in the 19th-century account says the male line ended with Johann Marschall von Hüneburg in 1369; the castle and office then passed through the Lichtenberg and Hanau lines. This history is useful context, not independent proof of the arms’ development ([Jahrbuch der K.-K. Heraldischen Gesellschaft Adler, digitized volume](https://knihovna.archives.cz/arl-zao/en/csg/?key=2059401912&repo=zaorepo)).

## Monument: Walter de Hunebourg, Niederhaslach

This is the strongest early monument lead and should be kept distinct from the seal claim. The state heritage record identifies a sandstone funerary slab in the old cemetery at Niederhaslach, 234 × 97 × 24 cm, bearing Walter de Hunebourg’s Latin epitaph and a canted heraldic shield. It dates the object to the first quarter of the 14th century (possibly 1315). The inventory describes the shield as *coupé* (per fess) with one swan head and neck in the upper part, then says that according to Kindler von Knobloch the arms had a second pendant head. The official record's `PHOTO` field is empty; I found no accessible photo in searches for the catalog number PM67001163. The monument therefore remains text-described, not image-verified.

**Image status:** the government record establishes the monument and reports the armorial carving, but the accessible page text provides no image that I could inspect in this research pass. Accordingly this is text-confirmed monument evidence, not image-verified proof of a pair or of outward orientation. The slab may be a critical bridge between an early crest pair and a later shield pair, but it cannot yet carry that conclusion on its own.

## Manuscript and armorial evidence; the transition question

The earliest well-described manuscript image located in this branch is **not the Grünenberg armorial**, but BNU Strasbourg ms. 739, fol. 5v. The catalog dates the codex's completion to not before 1391 and identifies the page's full arms as Wernher von Hüneburg's. Its description places one swan head in the shield's upper dexter and the pair in the crest. This provides direct manuscript evidence for a one-head shield plus paired crest by the end of the 14th century. The BNU/BNF image viewer was not retrievable in this pass (Gallica links returned 403/cache errors), so I rely on the specialist catalog's explicit image description and label it accordingly.

The Grünenberg image has now been isolated and visually checked. The visible caption at the bottom of p. 159 reads “Kraft von Hüneburg in Burgund”; the associated arms continue onto p. 160, where a single whole swan is in the red shield and the helm crest has two outward-facing swan necks. This supports the transcription's essential placement (single shield charge, pair in crest) and refines the shield figure from “swan head” to a whole swan in this image. The manuscript does **not** show two swans in the shield. The specialist index also argues that the earlier shield was plain per-fess and that one swan head entered the shield from the crest around 1270; c.1270 remains the index author's reconstruction, not an explicit manuscript date. ([BSB image 144](https://api.digitale-sammlungen.de/iiif/image/v2/bsb00035320_00144/full/full/0/default.jpg); [image 145](https://api.digitale-sammlungen.de/iiif/image/v2/bsb00035320_00145/full/full/0/default.jpg); [armorial.dk transcription and commentary](https://www.armorial.dk/german/Grunenberg.pdf)).

An additional and important scholarly source is Anja Rathmann-Lutz's study of the *Großes deutsches Memorial* in the digitized NARR volume [*Figuren der Referenz*](https://elibrary.narr.digital/xibrary/10.24053-9783772054761), especially its discussion of fol. 5v and the memorial-book programme (printed pp. 51–53; web text around lines 467–477). It independently specifies the shield as tilted, per fess sable and silver, with a silver swan neck in the dexter chief, and the crest as two silver swan bodies/neck forms turned away from one another. It also reports that the Memorial text describes Werner's grave and arms: Werner's arms had been painted above his stone sarcophagus; after acquiring the monastery in 1366, Rulman Merswin had the wall painting covered by a Resurrection image and the sarcophagus removed. This is significant context: the ms. 739 arms page is a retrospective memorial image made for the community, not a surviving 12th-century depiction or a contemporary seal. It preserves the institution's claimed founder-armorial identity, while its precision about the earliest historical blazon cannot be assumed without corroborating seals. The digitized account is more exact on the paired crest's orientation than a generic “two swan heads”: the two crest figures are *voneinander abgewandt* (turned away from one another). ([NARR study](https://elibrary.narr.digital/xibrary/10.24053-9783772054761); [KdiH catalog](https://kdih.badw.de/datenbank/handschrift/44/2/1)).

There is a second, concrete manuscript witness: Strasbourg, Archives départementales du Bas-Rhin, H 2184, the *Erste lateinische Memorial* (also called the *Zweites übriggebliebenes Lateinbuch*). KdiH says its last mirror leaf has a full-page image of Werner's arms in a design identical to BNU ms. 739. This moves the evidence beyond one isolated memorial page, although the two manuscripts belong to the same institutional memorial programme and so are not fully independent heraldic traditions. KdiH gives H 2184 as a 50-leaf parchment manuscript and cites Krusenbaum-Verheugen's illustrated detailed description. The ARCA result mentioning “f. 004v–005” was not established as a Hüneburg record and is excluded. For BNU ms. 739, the `Initiale` record lists f. 5v; the dating discrepancy remains: KdiH says completed not before 1391, while IRHT gives 1382–1385. ([KdiH memorials overview](https://kdih.badw.de/datenbank/untergruppe/44/2); [Krusenbaum-Verheugen, *Figuren der Referenz*](https://elibrary.narr.digital/xibrary/10.24053-9783772054761); [Initiale, BNU 739](https://initiale.irht.cnrs.fr/codex/11542); [KdiH, BNU 739](https://kdih.badw.de/datenbank/handschrift/44/2/1)).

The 19th-century account instead reports that the two-neck crest was later moved into the upper shield field, creating the familiar arms with two addorsed heads in chief. It does not supply the first dated seal or monument with both heads on the shield. Thus the safest chronological model is:

1. **Initial arms:** per-fess shield without swans (reported from older seals).
2. **Intermediate form:** one swan neck in the chief of the shield; two swan necks remain in the crest (reported in the Grünenberg tradition; the change is conventionally placed around 1270 by a modern armorial index).
3. **Later form:** two swan necks, addorsed/outward-facing, in the chief of the shield, while the paired crest remains. This transfer is reported retrospectively by the 19th-century account but is not yet anchored here to a securely dated primary image.

The 1315 Walter slab could show the transition, but the current catalogue description is not enough: it describes one charge and attributes the second to Kindler's reading. The Grünenberg index likewise does not establish the pair-on-shield form; it says one head in chief and a pair in the crest.

Schöpflin's *Alsatia illustrata Germanica Gallica* (1761), II, pp. 621–622 and plate “ad pag. 609,” is the earliest **dated published representation located so far** that is reported as showing two addorsed swan necks on the Hüneburg shield as well as two in the crest. Gabi Meier identifies that plate as showing the Alsatian Hüneburg arms with two outward-facing swan necks in the shield and a pair of left-facing necks in the crest ([Meier, note 228](https://www.zg.ch/behoerden/direktion-des-innern/amt-fuer-denkmalpflege-und-archaeologie/publikationen/ftw-simplelayout-filelistingblock-1/burgruine_huenenberg_im_kanton_zug_sbkam_48_reduziert.pdf)). The 19th-century *Adler* article also says the pair was later introduced into the shield. I have not independently viewed Schöpflin's plate itself, so 1761 is a text-verified printed terminus, not an image-verified one. Searches did not locate an earlier dated armorial image with the pair in the shield.

## Seals and source limits

Sigilla's 1248 type entry and attached seal image directly confirm the early arrangement: a per-fess shield and a helmet with a two-swan-neck crest. I inspected the 1248 impression and the separate, worn 1259 impression. The BSB Grünenberg pages now provide another direct visual check of the later medieval tradition: one whole swan in the shield, paired necks in the crest. A 19th-century synthesis says older seals kept the paired necks in the crest and that they were later transferred to the shield. The exact date of transfer remains untraced to a securely dated medieval seal or armorial with two heads in the shield.

### Walther's 4 April 1248 act: transaction, seal, and identification

The 1248 impression is attached to an extant parchment act, Strasbourg Archives municipales, shelfmark **3 AST 3 I / 58**. Sigilla transcribes the issuer's subscription as *Ego Waltherus marscalcus Argentinensis* (“I, Walther, marshal of Strasbourg”). The act says that Walther conveyed to Brother Helwig, prior, and the Strasbourg Dominican convent, for 80 marks of silver, his *curia* adjoining the property called *zen Wasen* in Strasbourg together with the patronage right of the chapel of St Bartholomew situated there. The act's corroboration formula states that he caused the instrument to be confirmed by the seals of his lord the bishop, the city of Strasbourg, and himself. Sigilla records three impressions in that order; Walther's is no. 3. The date is explicitly given in the act as the Saturday before Passion Sunday in 1248, converted by Sigilla to 4 April 1248. The catalogue links this to Wiegand's edition, *Urkundenbuch der Stadt Strassburg* I (1879), no. 321, pp. 240–241. ([Sigilla act record and transcription](https://sigilla.irht.cnrs.fr/103056); [Sigilla impression record](https://sigilla.irht.cnrs.fr/sigial.php/192145); [Wiegand edition citation](https://books.google.com/books?id=-bh12i5VKZ8C))

Sigilla describes Walther's seal type as a round, 65 mm equestrian war seal: a mounted knight with sword and a per-fess shield, his helmet crested with two swan necks. The legend is given as *S WALTHERI … ARGENTINENSIS*. The actual photograph of impression 3 confirms the layout: the shield is divided horizontally and bears no visible swans; the pair is mounted above the helmet as crest. The attachment record identifies green medieval wax, a pendant seal on green silk cord, no counterseal, and position 3 on the act. The act image and the seal image are independently viewable in the Sigilla galleries. ([seal-type description and dimensions](https://sigilla.irht.cnrs.fr/36441); [high-resolution seal image](https://sigilla.irht.cnrs.fr/iiris_img/private/image/3%20AST%203%20I%20-%2058-sc3.png); [act recto image](https://sigilla.irht.cnrs.fr/iiris_img/private/image/3%20AST%203%20I%20-%2058-r.JPG))

The instrument identifies this Walther securely by office and name, but it does not state his parentage. A later genealogical account in the *Jahrbuch der K.-K. Heraldischen Gesellschaft Adler* lists Werner of Hüneburg, who died in 1247, with a son Walther who was Strasbourg *Schultheiß* and a son Eberhard who was marshal of Hüneburg/Strasbourg in 1251; it also lists other younger sons. That account does **not** identify the 1248 marshal Walther as either the later Walther the *Schultheiß* or as a son of Werner. Indeed, the separate offices recorded in 1251 counsel against silently merging the men. The safest identification is therefore the Walther named in the act as bishop's marshal; his precise place in the family genealogy remains unresolved on the evidence reviewed here. ([Adler volume, genealogical passage and seal summary](https://knihovna.archives.cz/arl-zao/en/csg/?key=2059401912&repo=zaorepo))

The later seal impression of 1259 (Ad Bas-Rhin, G 1340/1) is associated by Sigilla with the same seal type, but that photographed impression is materially incomplete and does not offer a usable shield reading. It cannot establish a change from the 1248 design. Together with the 14th-century Hüneburg armorial tradition, the 1248 seal thus provides direct, dated evidence for the pair as a crest, and no positive evidence for a paired-swan shield. ([1259 impression and act description](https://sigilla.irht.cnrs.fr/223979))

Do not infer that every person called “von Huneburg” used these arms. The 19th-century literature itself distinguishes the Marshals of Hüneburg from another ministerial family named Hüneburg. The paired-neck heraldry discussed here is specifically tied by the armorial evidence to the Alsatian Marshalken of Hüneburg/Strasbourg.

## Conclusion for the two-outward-swan search

### Image-only audit after the Schweigern–Zwingenberg follow-up

For image requests and comparisons, the directly viewable evidence is as follows:

- **Walther de Hunebourg, 1248 seal impression:** [high-resolution Sigilla image](https://sigilla.irht.cnrs.fr/iiris_img/private/image/3%20AST%203%20I%20-%2058-sc3.png) and [Drive copy](https://drive.google.com/thumbnail?id=1TbwH9ZVipu7pHB2nWRlEFbSmdTvPJOFa&sz=w1600). This plainly shows the pair of swan necks above the helmet; the shield is per fess and the pair is in the crest.
- **Grünenberg, Kraft von Hüneburg:** [image 144](https://api.digitale-sammlungen.de/iiif/image/v2/bsb00035320_00144/full/full/0/default.jpg) and [image 145](https://api.digitale-sammlungen.de/iiif/image/v2/bsb00035320_00145/full/full/0/default.jpg). Read together, these show one whole swan in the shield and the paired outward-facing necks in the crest. They are not evidence for a two-swan shield.
- **BNU ms. 739, fol. 5v:** the [KdiH specialist description](https://kdih.badw.de/datenbank/handschrift/44/2/1) explicitly identifies the full-page image as Wernher von Hüneburg's arms, with one swan head in the upper shield field and two swan heads above the helm. The [Gallica manuscript object](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/btv1b10224576p) is the image source, but a direct folio image URL was not successfully retrieved in this pass; therefore this witness remains catalog-described rather than directly image-checked here.
- **Strasbourg AD, H 2184, final mirror leaf:** KdiH says the full-page arms image is identical in design to BNU ms. 739. The repository provides no page image in the sources accessed; count it as a specialist-catalog attestation, not an independently inspected picture.
- **Niederhaslach grave slab of Walter de Hunebourg:** the French Palissy record describes one swan neck on the shield and reports a second only through Kindler's reading. No photograph was available in the accessible record. Do not present this as a verified pair image.

This audit found no accessible monument photograph that clearly shows the Hüneburg swan pair. The 1248 seal remains the earliest direct, dateable image in this branch, and its placement is unambiguously a crest. The 1338 Schweigern/Zwingenberg leads do not supply another Hüneburg witness or alter the shield-versus-crest conclusion.

- **Confirmed family connection:** the Alsatian Marschalken von Hüneburg/Strasbourg have a long-attested two-swan-neck **crest**.
- **Confirmed later heraldic formulation:** 19th-century sources describe two addorsed/outward-facing swan necks in the chief of the shield, with a repeated pair in the crest.
- **Earliest pair image verified in this branch:** Walther de Hunebourg's seal impression attached to the 1248 act, with the paired heads in the crest, not the shield.
- **Earliest manuscript image described by a specialist catalog:** BNU ms. 739, fol. 5v, completed not before 1391; one head in the upper shield and two outward-facing necks in the crest. The actual digitization link is identified, but the image viewer could not be reached.
- **Independent scholarly confirmation:** Rathmann-Lutz's discussion of the same memorial image confirms the one-neck shield/pair-neck crest arrangement and the crest figures turned away from one another. She also ties the manuscript's arms image to the memorial community's account of Werner's tomb, rather than treating the 14th-century image as a contemporary 12th-century witness.
- **Second manuscript witness:** Strasbourg Archives départementales, H 2184, final mirror leaf, with a full-page Wernher von Hüneburg arms image in a design KdiH calls identical to BNU ms. 739. The specialist source verifies the witness; its page image was not checked in this pass.
- **Unresolved catalog result:** an ARCA record for an opening at f. 4v–5 could not be identified as a Huneburg depiction and is not counted.
- **Earliest shield charge:** one swan head in chief appears in the later 14th-century manuscript tradition, with c.1270 offered only as a modern reconstruction for the initial transfer of one head from crest to shield.
- **Earliest pair-on-shield representation located:** Schöpflin's 1761 plate, reported by modern literature to show two addorsed heads in the shield and a crest pair. The plate has not been independently visually inspected; no earlier dated armorial page or seal with this pair-on-shield arrangement was found.
- **Monument lead:** Walter de Hunebourg's c.1315 slab is officially catalogued with one swan neck in chief; the second is attributed to Kindler, but the French inventory has no photo and the pair/orientation remains unverified.
- **Research status:** the transfer account is supported in outline, but no medieval primary source examined here proves two swan heads on the Hüneburg shield. The secure early pair placement is the crest.

# Two swan necks in Franconian heraldry: regional findings

Research scope: the heraldic motif of paired swan necks/heads, especially the Uissigheim–Rosenberg tradition in Franconia, and the claim that the arms may have a blue field. This note distinguishes paired swan-neck crests from other swan arms that may look similar in seals or monuments.

## Main finding

The strongest securely documented regional match is the medieval heraldic group of the Lords of Uissigheim and the Rosenberg family. Their principal shield is a red-and-silver chequered/partitioned design; paired swan necks appear as a helmet crest. In the Rosenberg version, the necks are red and silver and flank a red rose. The evidence does **not** support blue as the field color for this particular group.

A local Uissigheim history page repeats a much wider list of families said to share the arms—Höchheim, Thalheim, Rannenberg/Kannenberg, Randersacker, Reinstein, and Rieneck—and proposes common descent. Treat this list as a lead, not as established fact. Independent descriptions conflict with it: Randersacker is described with a black wheel quarter in gold, while the well-documented Counts of Rieneck used red-and-gold bars and a single swan crest. Those families should not be identified as bearers of the paired-neck arms without checking their seals or monuments individually.

The blue-field clue may reflect a different nearby swan device. In Wolfert's regional survey, the related but distinct von Wipfeld arms are described as a silver swan's torso on blue, with a red crest and feathers. That is a swan body, not the paired swan-neck crest of Uissigheim/Rosenberg.

The local Uissigheim page quotes Werr's 1910 description of a tomb slab associated with Arnold III (d. 1336): the divided/striped shield and a helmet laid crosswise with two swan necks; it also mentions another such armorial relief embedded in Bronnbach church and a broken tomb slab in the cloister. This is useful monument evidence, but the same page warns that older local interpretations of Arnold's death are problematic. Use it for the physical heraldic description, not for unrelated historical claims.

## The wider-family list: caution and checking priorities

The claim that “the same arms” belonged to Höchheim, Thalheim, Rannenberg/Kannenberg, Randersacker, Reinstein, and Rieneck is reproduced on a Uissigheim community history page, which says it paraphrases the 1966 local history by Helmut Lauf and Otto Uihlein. The page provides no separate seal citations for each family. The phrase may conflate a regional kinship hypothesis, shared heraldic devices, and distinct arms.

- **Rieneck:** The *Historisches Lexikon Bayerns* (Theodor Ruf) identifies the arms as nine red/gold divisions and a **single standing swan** crest, adopted from the mid-13th century (first in the Rothenfels line, attested 1257/58); it passed to Hanau after 1559. This is a different heraldic system, not paired swan necks. Confidence: high.
- **Randersacker:** The available summary describes an entirely different shield, a black wheel quarter on gold, with a black/silver divided wing as crest. It also says the family was related to Kruse von Sande. This contradicts the broad Uissigheim claim. Confidence: medium pending verification against a primary seal or armorial image.
- **Höchheim, Thalheim, Rannenberg/Kannenberg, Reinstein:** no reliable, independent paired-neck blazon or seal was located in this search. The broad local claim is insufficient to assign the motif. “Reinstein” may itself be a name collision with the Regenstein/Harz comital arms; verify the exact family before linking it to Franconia.

This caution matters because “Schwanenhals” is a common crest element, while “two necks” as a crest is not itself proof of shared descent. The Rosenberg–Uissigheim connection has a specific combination of shield pattern, tincture reversal, name chronology, and related seal forms that is stronger than a generic motif match.

## Terminology and the blue-field question

Useful search terms in German include **zwei Schwanenhälse**, **Schwanenhälse als Helmzier**, **Schwanenrümpfe**, **zwei Schwanenköpfe**, and **Schwanenköpfe im Siegel**. In the cited regional evidence, Wolfert uses *Schwanenhälse* for the crest and *Schwanenrümpfe* for earlier seal images. *Doppel-Schwanenhälse* is a useful modern query phrase but was not the wording of the key source.

The distinction between necks, heads, and busts matters. A seal may show only paired necks/busts without the shield, and a later armorial may show the full helmet crest. A single swan (as in Rieneck) or a swan torso on blue (as in Wipfeld) should not be counted as the paired Uissigheim/Rosenberg device.

For the known Uissigheim/Rosenberg arms, the shield is red and silver, while the swan-neck crest is red and silver; the Rosenberg crest adds a red rose. No blue shield is reported in Wolfert's descriptions. The blue field in this geographic context belongs to a separate Wipfeld swan-body armorial tradition according to the same survey. This makes the visual memory of a blue ground understandable, but not evidence for blue in the Uissigheim/Rosenberg arms.

## Confidence summary

- **Uissigheim paired swan-neck crest; shield red/silver:** high.
- **Rosenberg related arms; color order reversed and rose between necks; seal sequence:** high, from Wolfert's detailed report of dated seals plus armorial and monument corroboration.
- **Common Uissigheim/Rosenberg family root:** medium-high; supported by specialist literature and heraldic/genealogical continuity, though this note does not inspect the underlying genealogical documents.
- **Blue ground for the paired-neck group:** low/unsupported; the best regional description says red and silver. Blue is supported for a distinct Wipfeld swan-body design.
- **Höchheim/Thalheim/Rannenberg/Reinstein as exact paired-neck arms:** low pending primary checks.
- **Rieneck as exact paired-neck arms:** contradicted by strong specialist description of a single swan crest and red/gold shield.

## Manuscript comparison: BSB Cod.icon. 307, image 0573

The BSB digitization of *Sammlung von Wappen aus verschiedenen, besonders deutschen Ländern* (Cod.icon. 307, catalogued c. 1600) identifies the compilation as a heavily used collection of 40 fascicles, with named family arms appearing within territorial and institutional groupings. The relevant scan is viewer image **0573** (the manuscript's own page number written at upper right is **617**): [open image 0573](https://api.digitale-sammlungen.de/iiif/image/v2/bsb00020245_00573/full/full/0/default.jpg). The gold caption directly below the shield is best read **“Stachl”**. An earlier gold note above the device is crossed out and too unclear to transcribe confidently; it is not enough to read the armorial entry as “Berckmayer.”

The shield and crest each show two paired, upward-curving forms. At first glance they can resemble swan necks, but the label makes a different identification more likely: Rietstap records a Bavarian **Stachel (Stachel von Stacheleck)** coat with an argent shield chaussé-ployé in gold and red, and a tournament cap crest surmounted by two *proboscides* (trunk-like charges) in gold and red. The agreement between the unusual name and repeated paired curved charges makes “Stachl” a plausible contemporary/variant label for this Stachel arms. This does **not** establish that the charges are swan heads. The BSB page is monochrome outline work with tincture letters, and the photographed shield's field appears unpainted; it supplies no evidence for a blue field. Rietstap's description is a later armorial attribution, so identification remains **medium confidence**, pending comparison with the actual Stachel grant or an independently identified family seal.

The archival finding aid for Hans Stachl (Stehle) von Dhaynow (grant dated 31 May 1559) records ennoblement, naming privilege, and a coat-of-arms privilege, but notes that the file contains no surviving coat drawing or seal. This confirms that a Stachl-named ennobled bearer existed by the period, but does not prove he is the bearer in Cod.icon. 307 or connect him to the older Stachel von Stacheleck family. The southern-Tyrolean Stachl/Stachelburg lineage is also a distinct possible name collision; do not merge it without evidence.

This example should therefore be cited as **“Stachl; probably paired proboscides, not securely swan necks”**. It is a useful cautionary parallel to the Uissigheim/Rosenberg material, not a confirmed additional swan-neck family.

## Cod.icon. 309 image supplied by user: identification limit

The BSB catalog title is *Wappen besonders von deutschen Geschlechtern* (Cod.icon. 309), usually dated 1475–1550 (some catalogs summarize it more broadly as 16th century). It is a multi-entry armorial of German families. The user's earlier image reportedly shows a blue shield with two neck/head forms and a swan crest, but no leaf or viewer-image number accompanied it. I could not securely match that cropped image to a particular labeled entry from the manuscript. Do not assign the bearer from motif alone. The image would become identifiable if its viewer image/folio is available; until then the shield color and apparent charge can be recorded as a visual observation, while name, family, and relationship remain unverified.

As a regional visual comparator only, Zwingenberg's historic arms are described as three silver swan necks with gold beaks on blue, and related local arms include Mülben and Waldkatzenbach. That is a **three-neck shield** and does not by itself identify the user's two-neck Cod.icon. 309 image. It does, however, independently confirm that blue fields with swan-neck charges occur in the broader Neckar-Odenwald border region.
